05 · His thesis, your proof

For each of his arguments, you have a working mechanism

He writes about organizations. You built a small one made of agents and hit the same problems. Keep the numbers exact (see the Answer Bank).

1 · “AI sandboxes are a new risk surface”

BrandonAgents with broad access break the security model. A model instruction isn't an enforcement boundary.
PapercuspControls are enforced at the boundary, in code: 47 editor hooks (9 run before any write) for file locks, secrets and generated-file guards. Authority is computed from a registry and checked when a tool is dispatched, not trusted from the prompt. Every long-lived process is confined to a cgroup and killed by task ID, never by name (#10 Security, N4).

2 · “Individual gains don't add up to organizational improvement”

BrandonIndividual engineers get 25 to 35% faster, but the organization doesn't.
PapercuspThat's exactly what happened. With about 100 agents on one branch, writing code stopped being the bottleneck. Integration became the bottleneck: a shared green gate, a red build that imported breakage faster than fixes landed, and a fix that froze the candidate and admitted repairs file by file (N2). Explain the coordination layer, not the agents.

3 · “Evidence, not vendor pitches”

BrandonHe rates the credibility of sources and dislikes unsupported claims.
PapercuspWork isn't marked done without structured evidence. Bug fixes need a hypothesis, an alternative explanation and a test that tells them apart. Graders are separate from implementers, and 47 doc-claim tests keep docs from drifting from the code (#8 Evaluating AI output).

4 · “Workflow redesign, not a technology purchase”

BrandonAdoption depends on people and workflows: training, trust and fewer tools.
YouYou're the human user of your own agent fleet, so you've learned what makes a person trust agents. Owner instructions are recorded word for word and closed with a stated outcome. There's an explicit “ask first” mode versus an “act and report” mode. One lesson: “rendered ≠ delivered.” Before that, you spent 12 years running a $1M+/month commerce business with a very small team by automating the workflow, not by buying tools.

5 · “The boring ML delivers the biggest returns”

BrandonDon't use an LLM where a rule or a regression does the job.
YouIn Papercusp, scheduling, locks, gates and liveness are deterministic code. The LLM is used only at judgment points. Be ready to say where you wouldn't use an LLM at Acadian.

07 · What he'll probably ask

Likely questions, with answer sketches

The posting tab already covers RAG and agent evals, where controls live, Bedrock vs. API vs. self-hosting, inference cost and cross-team disagreement. These are the ones Brandon is more likely to push on.

Design

“Build a research assistant over filings and earnings calls”

Start with the analyst's question and what counts as success. Ingest filings, transcripts and news with metadata such as ticker, date and source. Retrieval is filtered by entitlement before ranking. Answers must cite the exact passage, and the system says “I don't know” when retrieval is weak. Evaluate against a versioned set of known-answer questions, sliced by document type. Watch for look-ahead: anything used for backtesting must only see documents as of that date. Build on Bedrock, log everything, and roll out to a pilot desk first.

Security

“News and transcripts are untrusted input. Now what?”

Treat retrieved text as data, never as instructions. Don't put action-taking tools in the same context as untrusted content unless there's an approval step. Tools are on an allow-list with scoped credentials, and outputs are checked against a schema. Keep prompt-injection examples in the red-team eval set. This is his sandbox essay applied to the job.

Open weights

“Bedrock-hosted vendor model, or open weights?”

He has argued for open source in regulated shops, so take that seriously. Decide per workload: data classification, residency, auditability, how much fine-tuning is needed, cost at volume, and team capacity. A reasonable answer: use open-weights models (on Bedrock or on your own infrastructure) for sensitive or high-volume narrow tasks, and frontier APIs where quality matters most and the data is allowed to leave. Benchmark both on the real eval set.

Adoption

“How do you get portfolio managers and researchers to use it?”

Put it inside a workflow they already have. Give hands-on training (his 5-hours number). Keep to one or two tools, not many. Choose a small pilot group with a measurable outcome, look at usage and corrections every week, and treat abandoned features as bugs. Mention his own “5% vs. 95%” framing.

Influence

“You'll lead without formal authority. Show me how.”

Use #9 Technical disagreement: write down the shared outcome, compare two or three options with their failure modes, run a small test with a decision date, and make the exception path visible. Papercusp records rulings as “plan decisions” that other lanes read. That's a written-decision habit he'll recognize.

Honesty check

“How much AWS and Kubernetes day-two ops have you done?”

Answer with the exact scope you've actually run. Papercusp runs on Postgres, systemd and a Tauri desktop app, not on EKS. Say what carries over (on-call-style liveness, rollback, migrations, gates) and what you'd need to learn. He's an AWS Solutions Architect and will notice any stretching.

He is not screening your code — the earlier rounds did that. He is deciding whether you would be steady, and whether you would help him do the thing he keeps telling people he is going to do.

How the thirty minutes actually goes

Shape

The invitation says he will “give you a broader view of the organization, where engineering is headed, and how this team fits into the larger picture.” For a senior or staff IC, an EVP in the last slot is usually two things at once: a judgment-and-fit read, and a sell. That is good news — they do not spend an EVP’s half-hour on candidates they have already decided against — but it also means the last third of the call is where it is actually decided. Have three questions loaded before you dial in. Do not save them for minute twenty-eight.

The read on him

Signal → meaning

Everything on the left is his own public phrasing. The profile is unusually revealing — he writes about engineering in financial language, which most engineering leaders never do.

“Improved engineering CapEx capitalization from 40% to 60%, driving direct EBITDA impact.” Listed twice, across two roles
The single most revealing line on the profile. Capitalizing engineering labor rather than expensing it is a private-equity value-creation lever — it moves EBITDA on paper. Getting from 40% to 60% means he restructured how work is classified and tracked so more of it qualifies as new-product development rather than undifferentiated maintenance. Practical read: he responds to delivery described as plannable, project-shaped increments, and he is allergic to work that cannot be accounted for.
“$13M P&L” · “aligning spend with PE financial expectations” Current role

arrivia acquired ICE, where he had already spent fourteen years. He has been merging those two worlds since 2020. Your strongest card

Play it early

He has told the world three times that AI in the SDLC is his next transformation. Almost every candidate he meets will answer that topic with some version of “Copilot makes me faster.” You have actually run agentic engineering systems, including the parts that break. That is a rare credential for exactly this conversation, and it is worth spending real airtime on.

One posture note, because it is the main way this goes wrong for an IC candidate: answer as a practitioner with opinions, not as a consultant with a transformation plan. He owns the strategy; what he cannot get from his own leadership team is a credible first-hand account of what actually changes on the ground. Say “here is what I watched happen” rather than “here is what an organization should do.” The first is the rarest thing you can offer him. The second sounds like you are interviewing for his job.

“The naive version of AI in the SDLC is that engineers write code faster. That’s real, but it’s the small half. What actually changes is where the constraint sits. Generation gets cheap, so the bottleneck moves downstream — to review, to test coverage, to whether you can prove a change is correct before it ships. A team that adds AI without moving investment downstream just gets faster at producing unverified change.

And the failure mode isn’t code that obviously breaks. It’s confident wrongness — a change, or a status report, that reads clean and isn’t. So the thing you end up having to build is evidence-carrying completion: what ran, against which commit, with what output. ‘Done’ stops being an assertion and becomes a record.

The part I didn’t expect is how little of it was tooling. Access wasn’t the hard problem — agreeing on what counts as evidence was. And the number that turned out to matter wasn’t anything about code generated; it was how much work got redone.”

Roughly ninety seconds. Then stop and let him react.

Don’t say

“AI makes me about thirty percent more productive.” Everyone says it, nobody can substantiate it, and with a P&L owner it invites the one follow-up you can’t answer: measured how?

Say instead

Name one specific thing you changed about how work gets verified, and what went wrong before you changed it. Concrete failure stories are the only credible evidence anyone has in this area right now, and he will know that.

Hold one connection in reserve and do not volunteer it: AI in the SDLC shifts the mix between maintenance and new development, which is the exact lever behind his 40%-to-60% capitalization number. If he takes the conversation toward how the investment gets justified upward, having already seen that link is impressive. Raising it unprompted, as a candidate for an IC role, is the consultant move above.

What to ask him

The last ten minutes

Pick four — two from the first group, one from each of the others. Every one is answerable by him specifically, which is the point: a generic question signals you would have asked the same thing of anyone. Note that the first group is deliberately about the work rather than the strategy. For an IC, asking what the day actually looks like reads as seriousness; asking about the P&L reads as auditioning.

What the work is actually like
—
You consolidated two engineering organizations after the acquisition. Where has that actually landed — is the platform genuinely unified now, or are there still two stacks under the hood?
The single most informative question on this page. His answer tells you what your first year would be made of, and he will respect that you asked it plainly.
—
What does a senior engineer here actually own end to end — a service, a product surface, a partner integration?
Ownership scope is the thing that most determines whether a staff-level role is real or nominal, and it is rarely in the job description.
—
Right now, how much of engineering’s time goes to modernization and consolidation versus building new things?
The honest answer is usually uncomfortable, which makes it worth hearing. It also tells you whether the “unified platform” claim has shipped or is still in progress.
—
With partners at the scale of Amex and Hilton Grand Vacations, how much of what gets built is set by contractual commitment versus what the product team wants?
Shows you understand that enterprise partner platforms are not run like consumer products — and it is the single biggest predictor of what your backlog feels like.
To engage his agenda
—
You talk about AI across both product and the SDLC. Which one is further along here, and which one is turning out to be harder?
Splits his own framing back at him and makes a generic answer difficult. Ask this one if you ask nothing else in this group.
—
For AI in the SDLC — what is your measure that it is working?
Signals that you know measurement is the hard part, not adoption. Almost no candidate asks this, and it pairs directly with the answer on the previous section.
How you would be judged
—
A year from now, what would make you say this hire was clearly the right one?
Gets you the actual success criteria, and executives enjoy answering it. Write the answer down — it is the rubric.
—
Who would I be working for day to day, and how much has that layer moved during the consolidation?
Legitimate, neutral, and it quietly surfaces the risk in the next section without going near it.

One to be ready for, rather than to ask: he has been at the same company for over twenty years and was promoted four times inside it. Some version of “where do you want to be in three years” is likely, and to him a good answer involves still being there. Depth over title — owning something significant, going further into the domain — lands better here than a fast climb would.

One thing to weigh

Do not raise it

His public profile says he is looking to leave

The summary you sent ends: “I’m exploring CTO opportunities where I can lead engineering organizations through AI-native transformation.” That is on his public profile, so it is fair information — but never mention that you read it.

Two consequences. First, the person interviewing you may not be there in a year, which is a real thing to weigh if you would be joining partly because of him. The reporting-line question above is how you probe it without ever going near the subject.

Second, and more usefully: it tells you exactly what story he is trying to be able to tell about himself. He wants to have led an engineering organization through an AI-native transformation. A candidate who visibly helps make that true is more interesting to him than a candidate who is merely competent. That is not cynical — it is just knowing what he is optimizing for.

Domain ground to cover

arrivia’s actual business

arrivia builds white-label travel booking for other brands’ loyalty and membership programs — an Amex or T-Mobile member books travel and redeems points, and arrivia is the platform underneath. You do not need to be a travel expert, but being able to name the hard parts of this domain unprompted is a strong signal. These are the ones worth having a sentence about.

Points as a currency

Redemption pricing means dual-currency display, points-plus-cash splits, and a conversion rate that is a business decision rather than a technical one. It touches pricing, accounting and the partner contract at the same time.

Supplier integration

Inventory, rates and availability come from third parties over interfaces of wildly varying quality. He personally built REST and SOAP partner integrations, so this is home ground for him.

Stale rates and availability

The classic travel-platform problem: cache aggressively enough to hit OTA-grade search latency, and you will quote prices that no longer exist at booking time. Everything downstream is a consequence of that trade.

Booking idempotency

A retried booking that double-charges a member, or burns their points twice, is the failure that reaches the partner’s executive team. Worth being able to talk about in terms of correctness rather than just error handling.

Cancellations and refunds

Almost always harder than the booking path and almost always built second. If you want a question that shows domain instinct, ask which of the two is in worse shape.

The partner is the customer

The member books the trip, but Amex or T-Mobile signs the contract and defines the SLA. That split explains most of what looks strange about roadmaps at companies like this.

Before you reply to the scheduler

Logistics
  • They asked for the three in order. Pick times that keep the stated sequence intact — offering a slot that puts Shawn before the earlier two creates work for a coordinator and reads as inattentive.
  • All times quoted are Eastern. State your own timezone explicitly in the reply even if it is also Eastern. It costs one clause and removes the entire class of mis-booking.
  • Give more than the minimum. Two or three options per stage gets the loop booked in one round trip instead of three.
  • Ask what steps one and two are if you have not been told. At senior or staff level they are most likely a hiring-manager conversation and a technical round, which is where the actual evaluation happens — Shawn is the last slot, not the hard one. Knowing the order changes what you prepare first, and asking a coordinator is completely normal.
  • Thirty minutes means thirty minutes at EVP level. Have your questions in front of you and start asking around the eighteen-minute mark rather than waiting to be invited.

Mercor, full-time

Second track

Mercor describes itself as “organizing human intelligence to power the AI economy.” In practice: they recruit and vet domain experts — physicians, lawyers, engineers, consultants — and put that expertise to work for frontier AI labs, across four stated lines of business: find work, measure AI, build evals, and deploy agents. They also publish the APEX benchmark family, which tests “whether frontier AI models can perform economically valuable tasks” in professional services, medicine, and software engineering.

Why you fit this one unusually well

The angle

Here is the thing worth noticing: one of Mercor’s three published benchmarks is APEX-SWE — measuring whether frontier models can do software engineering. Their business is not adjacent to the question of how you verify AI-produced work. It is that question, sold as a product.

So the argument written out in the arrivia section — that generation was never the bottleneck, that the failure mode is confident wrongness, that “done” has to become a record rather than an assertion — is not a transferable talking point here. It is a position on their core problem, arrived at from the practitioner’s side rather than the vendor’s. Two of their four business lines are build evals and deploy agents. If you have run agents against real repositories and watched where the evaluation breaks down, you have first-hand data on something they sell. Lead with that rather than saving it.

The same answers will not work in both

Do not cross the wires

These two companies are close to opposites, and prepping them as one interview is the main risk in running both loops at once. What reads as maturity at one reads as the wrong instinct at the other.

Same story, different emphasis. The delivery example that lands at arrivia is the one where you called a slip early and protected the partner relationship. The one that lands at Mercor is the one where you shipped something underspecified and corrected it in flight. Both can be true of the same project — decide which half you are telling before you walk in.

On-site logistics

Settle these first
  • Travel, before you prepare anything else. Ask the coordinator to book the flight and hotel directly rather than reimbursing you — before you book anything yourself, and as a logistics question rather than a financial disclosure. A company that just closed $350M and pays out $4M a day can book a flight.
  • Ask for the agenda in advance. An on-site is a full loop, not a conversation: expect several back-to-back sessions and at least one working or technical block. Asking what the day contains is completely standard and it is the difference between preparing for the right thing and preparing for all of it.
  • Ask which office and what onsite means. They work in person from San Francisco, New York and London, and essentially every posting is tagged full-time onsite. Their benefits include a “proximity bonus” — annual housing support — which tells you they expect people to live near the office. If relocation is implied, find that out now rather than at offer stage.
  • Read one APEX writeup before you go. Their own benchmark for your own discipline is the cheapest possible preparation, and having an opinion on how they constructed it — especially where a benchmark like that would be misleading — is a far better conversation than anything you could say about their funding round.
  • Expect the day to be long. A full loop plus travel is a physical event, not just an intellectual one. Build in a buffer rather than flying in the same morning.

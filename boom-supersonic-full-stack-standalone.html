01 · The thesis

What they are actually buying

Read the verbs in the posting: own, sit with, decide, ship, design. Not "implement", not "support", not "gather requirements". They are buying a product engineer with the shortest possible path from a user's pain to a shipped tool, and they say it outright: "founder energy", "a blank repo to something users depend on".

Three profiles are fused into one hire:

  • A full-stack product engineer who can take a domain from the data model through the UI without handing off.
  • Someone who understands that manufacturing data is not CRUD. Part designs are configuration-controlled, physical units are traceable, and the build record is legal evidence.
  • An API designer who treats agents as a first-class consumer. They build MCP servers, schemas, and APIs, with "no pre-AI architecture to retrofit".

The seam is speed versus correctness, and the strong answer draws the line precisely instead of choosing a side:

Ship it by FridaySame iteration speed — but never skip attribution, immutability, or review
Search, lookup, where-used, dashboardsReleasing a part revision or an engineering change
Eliminating copy-paste between systemsWriting the as-built record of a serialized unit
UI iteration, workflow shortcuts, reportsInspector buyoffs, nonconformance dispositions
Read-only agent toolsAnything touching export-controlled technical data

The line to land: "Read paths ship on Friday. Write paths to the record of truth iterate just as fast, but they are append-only, attributed, and never optimistic. That split is what lets you move fast in aerospace without becoming the reason an audit fails."

03 · Opener

Ninety seconds that answer the posting

"I build systems where the user is sometimes a person and sometimes an agent. Most recently that's Papercusp, a multi-agent engineering platform where a fleet of AI agents and humans share one codebase, one database, and one tool layer. The interesting problems were never the prompts. They were the ones any shared system of record has: who changed what, which copy is the truth, and what happens when two writers collide."

"The lesson I'd bring to Boomie is that an API that's good for agents is almost always a better API for people. Bounded responses, errors that tell you how to fix the call, a dry run before any consequential write, and every write attributed to someone. We got there by watching agents fail, and every fix made the human experience better too."

"What draws me here is doing that on data where being wrong has physical consequences, like the record of what's installed in serial number seven, and doing it by sitting next to the people who use it. Ship something by Friday, and still be able to prove what was built. That's the combination I want."

Adapt this to your real evidence. It is a frame, not a claim about your history. Pull every Papercusp specific from the Papercusp Answer Bank, where the numbers were re-checked against the tree. Claim only what you personally designed or built.

05 · Vocabulary

The aerospace software stack, in one table

"Six clicks to find a part number" and "twenty minutes a day copying part numbers between systems" describe one structural fact. The part number is the join key across three or four systems, and each system holds its own copy. Everything below hangs off that.

06 · Data model

Parts, BOMs, and the unit you can hold in your hand

If they give you a design problem, expect it here. The trap is modeling the BOM as a tree of part numbers. It is a DAG of revisions, conditioned by effectivity, and it is a separate structure from the tree of physical units actually built.

part           (id, part_number UNIQUE, name, make_or_buy, export_class)
part_revision  (id, part_id, rev, status: draft|released|obsolete, eco_id)
bom_edge       (parent_rev_id, child_rev_id, qty, find_no,
                effective int4range)          -- serial-number effectivity
eco            (id, number, reason, state, released_at, released_by)
unit           (id, part_rev_id, serial_no, lot_no)        -- a physical thing
install        (parent_unit_id, child_unit_id, work_order_step_id,
                installed_at, removed_at, recorded_at, recorded_by)
  • Surrogate keys. The part number is a natural key with a history. Part numbers get re-identified, and a primary key must never change.
  • Design and physical are separate models. bom_edge is as-designed and install is as-built. Conformity is the diff between them. That one sentence tends to win the design round.
  • Append-only, bitemporal. Never UPDATE a released revision or an install row. Supersede it. Keep both times: when it was true (installed_at / removed_at) and when it was recorded (recorded_at). That lets you answer "what did we believe on the day we shipped serial seven?"
  • Export classification is a column, not a folder. It decides which people, and which agents, can read the row (§08).
  • The ECO is first-class. Every released revision points to the change that released it.

The core query: explode a BOM for a specific serial number, with rolled-up quantities and a cycle guard:

WITH RECURSIVE explode AS (
  SELECT e.child_rev_id, e.qty::numeric AS qty, 1 AS depth,
         ARRAY[e.parent_rev_id, e.child_rev_id] AS path
    FROM bom_edge e
   WHERE e.parent_rev_id = $1
     AND e.effective @> $2::int                 -- effective for this serial
  UNION ALL
  SELECT c.child_rev_id, x.qty * c.qty, x.depth + 1, x.path || c.child_rev_id
    FROM explode x
    JOIN bom_edge c ON c.parent_rev_id = x.child_rev_id
   WHERE c.effective @> $2::int
     AND c.child_rev_id <> ALL (x.path)        -- a cycle is a data error; don't loop on it
)
SELECT child_rev_id, sum(qty) AS total_qty, max(depth) AS max_depth
  FROM explode
 GROUP BY child_rev_id;
-- Postgres 14+ also has a native  CYCLE child_rev_id SET is_cycle USING path  clause.
-- Where-used is the same walk in reverse: index bom_edge(child_rev_id).
"The as-built record for unit 12 shows a part at rev C, but the traveler called for rev B. How does your system prevent that, or at least make it loud?"

The install is a scan against the work-order step, never a typed entry. The write validates the scanned unit's revision against the planned MBOM effective for this serial. On a mismatch, it neither records silently nor blocks silently: it refuses the install and opens a draft NCR pre-filled with the unit, the step, the expected rev, and the found rev, so the exception enters the real workflow. The history is append-only, so the record shows who scanned what, when. If rev C is genuinely interchangeable, that is an effectivity or MRB decision made by someone with authority, not a data-entry override.

"Where-used for part X across every active build. How fast, and how do you keep it fast?"

Reverse traversal on an index over child_rev_id. At the scale of one aircraft program (thousands to tens of thousands of parts), a recursive CTE in Postgres is typically fast enough, so measure before you cache. If it gets hot, add a closure table (ancestor, descendant, qty) that is rebuilt on ECO release. That is the only time the design graph changes, and it makes invalidation trivial. Say the measure-first part out loud; it is the instinct they're screening for.

"Diff two revisions of a BOM."

Key children by (find_no, child part), not by position, then classify each as added, removed, quantity changed, or revision changed. Report revision-only changes separately. They are the interchangeability question in disguise, and the reviewer will care about them more than about a quantity change.

07 · Systems of systems

The first tool in daily use is almost certainly a read path

The twenty-minutes-a-day copying problem is usually a lookup problem wearing a data-entry costume. A single search across PLM, ERP, and MES, joined on part number, with where-used and current stock alongside, removes the copying without touching a single write path. It is low risk, it gets used daily, and it earns you the trust you'll need for the write paths later.

  • Source of truth per field, not per system. PLM owns the revision, ERP owns on-hand quantity, MES owns install state. Write it down; most integration bugs are two systems each believing they own the same field.
  • Sync you don't reconcile is sync you can't trust. Use idempotent sync keyed on (system, record, version), plus a reconciliation job that reports disagreements rather than silently picking a winner.
  • Strangler fig. Wrap the vendor system before you replace it. Put an anti-corruption layer between the two so the vendor's data model doesn't leak into Boomie's.
  • Every integration has a freshness contract. Say what "current" means for each field: real-time, a minute, or overnight. A stale inventory count with a timestamp is fine; one without a timestamp is a bug.
WeekPlanEvidence you produce
1Sit with three users. Stopwatch the workflow. Ship one small fix to anything they touch.Code in production on day 3–5. A written list of their workarounds.
2Pick the one pain with the biggest minutes × people. Build a v0 read path and put it in one person's hands.One named user using it.
3Iterate daily with that person. Add a second user. Instrument usage.A daily-active count.

The embedding technique: watch, don't interview. People describe the official process. What you need is the workaround: the spreadsheet on the desktop, the sticky note on the monitor, the second browser tab they keep open. The workaround is the spec. Saying this, with a real example, is the strongest answer to "comfort embedding with non-engineering users".

08 · Humans and agents

"Equal footing" has a concrete test

Can an agent do everything the UI can, through the same API, with the same permissions and the same audit trail? If the UI calls a private endpoint that the agent can't, you have built two systems, and the agent one will rot. The UI should be one client of the API, and the agent another.

PrincipleWhy agents need itWhy humans benefit too
Bounded responses, with field selectionContext is finite; a 50 KB response is unusableFaster pages, predictable payloads
Reject unknown arguments; never ignore them silentlyA silently ignored filter reads as successA typo'd filter can't quietly return the wrong parts
Errors that name the fixThe agent corrects itself on the next callThe message a support engineer needed anyway
Dry run by default for consequential writes; explicit confirmThe agent can preview an effect before committing it"What will this ECO release change?" before it does
Idempotency keysAgents retryDouble-clicks, and flaky Wi-Fi on the floor
Every write attributed: human, agent, and on whose behalfAccountability for delegated actionsThe AS9100 audit trail, for free
"Unknown" is a value, never coerced to false or zeroAgents act on defaultsA dashboard showing 0 when it means "not measured" misleads people too
Discovery instead of dumping the whole catalogA large tool catalog doesn't fit in contextSame problem for a new hire

This is where Papercusp is direct evidence. Its tool layer does each of these, and several exist because agents failed without them. That makes it a story about learning, not a list of features. Take exact figures, such as catalog size, from the Answer Bank, not from memory.

A sketch for the parts domain. Keep read tools broad and write tools narrow: agents draft, people confirm.

resources  part://{part_number}@{rev}          read-only snapshot, cacheable
           unit://{serial}/as-built

tools      parts.search(q, filters, limit, fields)
           bom.explode(part_rev, serial?, depth?)          effectivity-aware
           bom.where_used(part_rev, active_only = true)
           conformity.diff(serial)                         as-designed vs as-built
           ncr.draft(unit, step, description, photos[])    returns the NCR it WOULD open
           ncr.submit(draft_id, idempotency_key)           requires a human confirmation

Agents act with the delegating user's permissions, never through a service account that can see more than that person can.

The agent question that matters most in aerospace: export-controlled data. ITAR/EAR technical data sent to a model endpoint that isn't authorized for it can itself be an export problem. So the classification has to travel with the data through the agent layer. Rows carry their export class; the MCP server filters on the caller's authorization and on which model endpoint is on the other end of the session; tool results are tagged. Don't assert how Boom handles this. Ask (§12). Most candidates won't think to raise it, and it shows you understand where agents meet this industry.

09 · Frontend

A defensible state decision, and the factory floor

"Defend an API design and a frontend state decision in the same conversation." Have one decision ready in this shape: the decision → the alternatives → what forced it → what it cost → what would change your mind.

  • User-meaningful state lives in the URL, not in component state. That covers view, selection, filters, and which panel is open. Links become shareable, the back button works, and an agent can read and set the UI's state. Papercusp enforces this (nuqs), and the reason recorded there is Boom's reason exactly: state held in useState is invisible to agents. The cost is some URL design discipline and care with large selections.
  • Server state and UI state are different species. Server data goes through one cache and sync layer. Hover, focus, and mid-edit drafts stay local.
  • Push versus poll. Papercusp syncs over SSE everywhere, with polling only as a degraded fallback: many live queries polling against a browser's per-host connection cap starve each other. Take the exact figures from the Answer Bank.
  • Optimism has limits. An optimistic update is right for a filter and wrong for an inspector's buyoff. A buyoff shows as recorded only when the server has it.
Floor constraintDesign response
Tablets, glovesBig targets, few steps, nothing that needs precision tapping
Barcode scanners (keyboard-wedge input)A scan is accepted from anywhere on the screen, validated immediately, and never lands in whatever field happened to have focus

10 · AI dev tools

Where they help, where they don't

"AI made writing software cheap. It didn't make knowing it's correct cheap. The job moved to verification."

That line agrees with Scholl's own thesis (AI makes custom tools affordable) and adds the part aerospace cares about. It is an opinion, which is what the posting asks for.

Where they helpWhere they hurt
Scaffolding forms, CRUD, and internal tools users can react to by FridayConfident claims about numbers nobody traced back to the code that writes them
Translating between schemas and vendor export formatsTests that have never failed, which is a false green (prove a test can fail before trusting it)
Exploring an unfamiliar API or codebase quicklyLibrary APIs remembered from a stale training cutoff (pull current docs)
Mechanical refactors across many filesData migrations on records of truth
Test scaffolding and fixture generationAnywhere "plausible" and "correct" diverge, which describes most of configuration management

Your evidence is unusual: you have operated fleets of agents on a shared codebase, not just used an assistant. Have one concrete "we learned this the hard way" ready: a guard that had to be proven falsifiable, a metric agents kept misreading until the rule became "read the code that writes it". Also have one example of changing your mind about a tool. The posting explicitly wants "strong opinions, held loosely".

11 · Drills

Say these out loud once

Boom hasn't published its interview loop, so ask the recruiter. The posting suggests you should prepare for three things: practical full-stack coding, designing a Boomie domain, and a conversation about users and judgment.

"Tell me about taking a blank repo to something people depended on."

Cover who used it, the moment you knew they depended on it (usually: they complained the day it broke), what you cut to ship it, and what you'd do differently. Dependence is proven by breakage, not by praise.

"Tell me about a time you were proven wrong."

They wrote "rather be proven wrong today than right in six months", so they will ask. The strong shape: you said it fast, you corrected the record where others would read it, and you changed something so that class of mistake can't recur. The recurrence guard is what separates a senior answer.

"A user asks for feature X. After watching them, you think they need Y. What do you do?"

Build the smallest version of Y that also solves the pain behind X, put it in their hands, and let their use settle the argument. Avoid a stakeholder debate. The posting spends a paragraph rejecting the review-board model; don't rebuild it in your answer.

"Why not a big OEM, or configuring enterprise software?"

Configuration optimizes for the vendor's model of your business. Building optimizes for the tech in front of you. Make the point without sneering at the vendors: they solved real problems for a different era and a different cost of software.

Design: universal part search across PLM, ERP, and MES

Clarify freshness needs per field, source of truth per field, and export-control filtering. Rank exact part-number matches first, then prefix and description. Show where-used and on-hand stock inline. Use an index fed by change events, with a reconciliation report. Ship the read path first.

Design: a digital traveler for a floor tablet, with offline buyoffs

Steps and required buyoffs come from the MBOM and routing. Scan-to-install is validated against effectivity. Buyoffs queue offline with idempotency keys, and are never shown as final until the server confirms. Nonconformances branch into a draft NCR. The record is append-only.

Design: "which build slots are at risk from late purchase orders?" as an agent-facing tool

Join the build schedule to the MBOM and then to open POs and supplier promise dates. Return bounded, ranked results that state their reasoning. Treat unknown promise dates as unknown, not on-time. Expose it as a read tool; the agent drafts expedite requests, and a person sends them.

Code warm-ups

BOM explosion with rolled-up quantities and cycle detection. Where-used. Diffing two BOM revisions. A topological sort for build order. An idempotent endpoint that survives a retried request. Do each once in the language you'd use live.

12 · Questions

Ask things that change your decision

  • "Which Boomie domain would I own first, and who's the person I'd sit with in week one?" This tells you whether "embed with users" is real.
  • "Does Boomie replace 3DEXPERIENCE or wrap it? Where does the released EBOM live today?" This tells you whether the job is building or integrating.
  • "Superpower deliveries are targeted for 2027. Is the turbine production ramp Boomie's most urgent consumer right now?"
  • "Symphony and Superpower share most of their hardware. How do you handle configuration control when one part number serves an FAA program and an industrial product?"
  • "How do agents meet ITAR-controlled data today? Which model endpoints are authorized, and who decides?"
  • "Who are the agent consumers today: engineers' coding agents, internal copilots, or autonomous workflows?"
  • "How do you know a tool is 'in daily use'? What's one that didn't make it?"
  • "When Boomie's records feed certification evidence, how is the software itself validated?"
  • "Where would I be based, the Denver-area HQ, Greensboro, or both, and how much time on the floor?" Embedding implies being on site.
  • "What level are you evaluating me for?" The title says Software Engineer and the posting describes Senior. The bands differ by $50k at the midpoint.

02 · The company

Braid in 90 seconds

Founded in 2020, based in Shibuya, about 30 people from 20+ countries. Their tagline is “From intent to manufacturable geometry through automated reasoning.”

Product

AURA, a design “reasoning engine”

In: functional goals, physical conditions, a geometric envelope, the manufacturing process, and cost or weight targets. They call this spec “the contract AURA must satisfy.” No library of past designs is needed.

Out: 3D solid CAD, several valid alternatives with different trade-offs (“including geometries engineers would not have drawn unaided”), and an auto-generated PDF report for each.

Claim: “hours rather than weeks.”

Architecture

Neuro-symbolic

A symbolic layer encodes physics laws, geometric structure and manufacturing limits into the program that generates geometry. A shape that breaks a physical law or manufacturing limit “cannot be produced.” Learned models guide the search by proposing directions and ranking partial solutions, but they don't create the geometry.

“The symbolic layer makes AURA correct, the machine learning layer makes it efficient.”
Workflow

Human → machine → human

  1. Define (engineer). This is where judgment “matters most.”
  2. Reason (AURA). Physics, geometry and manufacturing are “considered together,” not checked one after another.
  3. Review (engineer). See which constraints are binding and which have slack.
  4. Decide (engineer). The chosen design goes on to simulation, prototyping and production.
Positioning

What they're arguing against

Today's tools are disconnected: “CAD knows nothing about physics. Physics knows nothing about manufacturing.” Generative design usually optimizes a seed shape, then someone makes it buildable by hand, “often negating the performance that was gained.” Braid designs de novo, so the geometry is “manufacturable from the first output.”

Founders

Three founders, three disciplines

  • Ivo Timóteo, CEO. ML researcher and principal lecturer for Cambridge's AI II course at 25; later CTO of a self-driving startup with about 60 engineers.
  • Guido Cossu, R&D and technology. See below.
  • Yuta Murakami, COO. Former McKinsey partner in manufacturing, supply chain and logistics across North Asia.

Advisors include Pritzker-winning architect Kazuyo Sejima. The team says it shares her ideal of uniting “functionality, ergonomics and aesthetics,” which explains “Creative Director” in Guido's title.

04 · Why you

The bridge: Papercusp already runs on Braid's principle

Don't pitch Papercusp as “AI agents.” Pitch it as a deterministic system that keeps probabilistic proposers correct. Braid is built the same way.

1 · Proposers vs. an enforcing layer

BraidML proposes search directions and ranks partial solutions. The symbolic layer guarantees physics and manufacturability.
PapercuspLLM agents propose actions. Typed tool schemas, enforced file locks, dispatch-time authority checks and evidence-gated completion decide what actually happens. Rules are enforced in code, not left to the prompt.

2 · One contract, compiled to many targets

BraidThe spec is “the contract AURA must satisfy.” Constraints become the program that generates geometry, so “the constraint set determines what the search can express.” The 2025 HN post described this as “compilers for physics problems.”
Papercusp“The tool contract is defined once and projected across MCP, HTTP and IPC” (from your email). Docs and prompts are generated from sources, so derived data can't drift from its source.

3 · Invalid states can't be produced

BraidCorrect by construction instead of generate-then-repair. A shape that violates the rules is never produced.
PapercuspA green gate that only fast-forwards main on a passing suite, locks that block rather than warn, and ledgered work that isn't “done” without evidence. You moved checks from after the fact to up front.

4 · Coupled parts, ordered generation

BraidTopological order over a liaison graph: anchors first, and each later part treats finished neighbors as fixed. Tightly coupled pairs iterate.
PapercuspPlans with blocked-by dependency graphs; parallel agents serialized on shared files; a scheduler that orders work by dependencies. It's a different domain with the same scheduling problem. Offer this as an analogy, not a claim of geometry expertise.

5 · Physical-world operations

Twelve years running a commerce company with a warehouse: over $1M a month with two engineers and three warehouse staff, about 90% under normal operating overhead, because nearly everything was automated. This answers “fascination with the physical world” with real inventory, fulfillment and logistics, and it gives you common ground with Yuta's supply-chain background.

Don't overclaim: you are not a computational geometry, FEM or physics expert, and Guido will know within one question. Say it before he asks: “My depth is systems architecture and making unreliable components behave reliably. Geometry and simulation I'd ramp into, and your careers page says you'll help with that.”

Questions he's likely to ask

Why Tokyo? Are you ready to relocate?

Answer directly with the decision you made in section 01. If yes, give a timeline and what it depends on. If you're unsure, say what would decide it. Don't be vague. Mention that you've read their Tokyo guide.

How do you think about LLMs and formal methods?

This checks the careers page line about deep learning not being the only legitimate tool. Your honest view from building Papercusp: LLMs are excellent proposers and unreliable executors. You kept them inside typed contracts, enforced locks and evidence gates, and moved every rule you could from the prompt into code. His white paper makes the same point about LLMs needing MCPs, APIs or formal languages for exact reasoning, so say you agree and that you've built on MCP.

What's the hardest systems problem you've solved?

Choose one and tell it as constraint → mechanism → measured result. Candidates: many agents safely editing one working tree (enforced locks plus a background sync that commits and merges); a release gate that keeps main green while many agents push; one tool contract projected to three transports. Give a number (agents, items, time saved) and one thing you got wrong and fixed. He values honesty over a polished story.

How would you design the pipeline from an engineer's spec to solver jobs?
  1. Typed spec / IR: units attached to quantities, load cases and manufacturing process as data, not free text.
  2. Well-posedness pass: reject specs that are over- or under-constrained, missing load cases, or have mismatched units, before any compute runs. His paper says problems must be “well-posed before execution.”
  3. A DAG, not a script: compile the spec into a dependency graph of solver tasks (his paper: “compiles dynamic computational graphs”).
  4. Content-addressed cache: key results on the hash of inputs plus solver version, so re-runs cost nothing and results are reproducible.
  5. Fidelity ladder: screen with surrogates, confirm finalists with high-fidelity solves, and treat non-convergence as a result, not a crash.
  6. Provenance → report: generate the PDF report from recorded provenance (which constraint bound, which solver, which version). Never hand-write it. This is Papercusp's derive-don't-hand-maintain rule.
  7. Durable execution: jobs survive restarts; Papercusp uses durable workflows for this.
How do you work with little supervision?

Braid says meetings are “rare, deliberate, and never scheduled by default” and “deep, quiet execution is our primary operating mode.” Give an example of choosing your own direction and making progress visible: written plans, decision records, frequent checkpoints. That connects to his post about weekly progress reports.

Tell me about a time you were wrong.

Rigor and honesty are on their list. Pick a real case where a measurement overturned your assumption, such as a metric you misread or a cause you blamed too early, and describe the guard you added so it couldn't happen again. Keep it short and specific.

07 · Enough to follow, not to bluff

Technical crib sheet

If he goes into this territory, understand it well enough to ask a good follow-up question. Don't present it as your own expertise.

Neuro-symbolic, by analogy

A familiar public example is DeepMind's AlphaGeometry: a language model proposes auxiliary constructions and a symbolic engine does the proof. AURA follows a similar pattern, with learned models ranking and a symbolic program generating. (This analogy is ours; Guido's paper doesn't cite it.)

Topology optimization vs. Braid

The standard approach is density-based topology optimization (for example, SIMP). It produces organic shapes that often need manual rework to be stamped or cast. Braid's point is that manufacturing rules are built in from the start, as with the draw-depth limit that ties the beam's depth to the panel in the car door.

Shape derivatives (their Hadamard post)

How an objective changes when you push a boundary. Hadamard's result: for smooth shapes, only the normal component of the boundary motion matters. The adjoint method gives the gradient with respect to all design variables for about one extra solve.

JAX (their JAX post)

jit traces Python code into a jaxpr, which is lowered to XLA and compiled. Python control flow on traced values fails, so you use lax.cond or lax.scan. New shapes or static arguments trigger recompilation. grad and vmap are transformations of the jaxpr.

Stamping vocabulary

Draw depth (how deep a sheet can be pressed), minimum radii, uniform sheet thickness (1.0 mm panel and 1.5 mm beam in the study), and body-in-white (the car's unpainted structural shell). The two load cases in the study were door sag and over-opening, each limited to 1.5 mm displacement at the latch.

Limitations they state themselves

The car door was “an exploratory study rather than a customer deployment”: two parts, one process (stamping), two static load cases, and no crash simulation. Mentioning this respectfully shows you read the study closely.

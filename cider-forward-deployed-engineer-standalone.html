If you have five minutes

Your central message: “I take responsibility for the outcome—from understanding the client's problem to deciding what is worth doing, delivering it, and making sure people use it.”

Prepare three specific examples

  1. A client conversation where the original request changed after discovery.
  2. A production AI feature: users, your personal contribution, evaluation, and an operational failure.
  3. An end-to-end delivery with a business result you can substantiate.

Lead with judgment

  • Ask what decision or workflow needs to improve before naming a technology.
  • Offer build, implementation, advisory, or audit based on evidence.
  • Explain cost, uncertainty, and adoption in plain language.
  • Separate AI in the delivered product from AI used to develop it.

Diagnose the engagement before pricing a build

A hot lead has a problem and an initial theory. Your job is to test that theory. These four paths come from the posting; an engagement can move between them after an explicit decision.

Build

Custom software is justified

The workflow and success measure are clear, and existing products leave a meaningful gap.

Deliver: a bounded production capability, acceptance tests, deployment, operating ownership, and adoption plan.

Example: a specialized approval workflow across several systems, with permissions and audit requirements that the current tools cannot support.

Hands-on AI implementation

The missing piece is making available tools work

An existing platform can do much of the job, but data access, configuration, integrations, controls, or staff training are missing.

Deliver: a configured workflow, representative evaluation, integrations, training, and a named operator. Small amounts of custom code may still be needed.

Example: connect an approved knowledge assistant to a curated document collection with access control and feedback.

Advisory / consulting

The client needs a decision and can execute it

The important facts are available and the client has implementation capacity. The value is judgment, options, or an architecture review.

Deliver: a recommendation with reasons, tradeoffs, sequence, and a decision owner.

Example: compare a vendor product with a custom approach and recommend the lower-risk path.

Paid audit

There is not enough evidence to commit

Several automation opportunities compete, or the economics, data quality, and workflow are unknown.

Deliver: a workflow inventory, measured baseline, ranked opportunities, constraints, estimated value/cost ranges, and a go/no-go recommendation.

Example: observe operations and sample cases to establish where automation would actually save money.

A useful redirect: “I can help you evaluate that build. Before you commit to it, I'd like to check whether the bottleneck is the software, the underlying data, or the process. Then I can recommend the smallest engagement that gets you a useful result.”

Run the first conversation

Practice prompt: “We spend too much time answering customer requests. Can you build us an AI agent?” This is a hypothetical lead, not a Cider project.

  1. Outcome · first 3 minutes. “What is happening that made this urgent? Is the problem response time, errors, staff effort, lost sales, or something else? What would be different if this worked?”
  2. Observe the workflow · next 5 minutes. “Walk me through the last real request. Who touched it? Which systems did they open? Where did they wait? Show me a difficult case as well as a routine one.”
  3. Measure · next 4 minutes. “How many requests arrive? How long does handling take, including rework? How often are answers wrong? What happens when a request is delayed? Do these numbers come from records or estimates?”
  4. Constraints · next 4 minutes. “Which data can we use, and who is allowed to see it? Which actions require approval? What systems and contracts must stay? Who will maintain the result? What budget and decision date should we work within?”
  5. Decision · last 4 minutes. Restate the problem in the client's language. Separate facts from assumptions. Recommend the next engagement, its deliverable, its cost boundary, and the decision it will unlock. Name the sponsor and next meeting.

If the client cannot provide a baseline

Propose a small paid diagnostic: inspect a representative sample, time the current process, interview users, and identify access constraints. Explain the questions it answers and set a stop date. Do not turn unknowns into a confident fixed implementation quote.

If the client insists on the original solution

Respect their goal, show the assumption your recommendation challenges, and offer a limited comparison. “Let's try both on the same sample and decide using handling time and errors.” If you cannot support their desired outcome honestly, narrow the promise or decline that scope.

Close-back: “The expensive part appears to be finding the right information, while sending the answer is quick. I'd first test an assistant that drafts a cited answer for your team. If that does not improve the whole workflow, we should not expand it into autonomous customer replies.”

Turn technical choices into a business proposal

A one-page proposal structure

  1. Problem and baseline: who is affected, current volume/time/errors, and evidence quality.
  2. Options: improve the process, adopt/configure a product, or build; include doing nothing.
  3. Recommendation: why this option earns its cost, and the assumption most likely to change it.
  4. Scope: deliverables, exclusions, data/access dependencies, and client responsibilities.
  5. Commercials: effort and cost range, billing assumptions, operating costs, milestones, and a change-control rule.
  6. Acceptance and adoption: measurable thresholds, decision owner, rollout, training, support, and a go/no-go date.

State uncertainty without sounding vague

“The integration is the largest unknown. I can bound discovery now. After we inspect the API and test a sample, I'll give you an implementation range with its assumptions. If we encounter a new dependency, you'll see the cost and options before we expand the scope.”

Change request: describe what changes, the impact on the existing goal, cost/date options, and what gets deferred. Get a decision from the client before spending against new scope.

Design a small production system you can own

Continue the hypothetical customer-request case: build a staff-facing assistant that retrieves approved information, drafts an answer with citations, and requires a human to send it. Earn broader autonomy through evidence.

Staff UI→Authenticated API→Authorized retrieval→Draft + citations→Human approval→Audited CRM action

Choose the smallest useful mechanism

Rules, RAG, or an agent?

Use rules for stable structured decisions. Use RAG when the answer needs current or private source material. Use bounded agent tool selection when the next step varies and that flexibility improves the outcome. A single retrieval-and-draft workflow may be enough.

Keep critical business rules and permissions in application code. Never let a retrieved document redefine the system's instructions.

Own front to back

Application, data, and operations

Start with a maintainable application boundary: UI, authenticated API, relational records, document ingestion, and background jobs where needed. Add tenant and user authorization to retrieval and actions. Use scoped credentials, idempotency for writes, bounded retries, and explicit failure states.

Show the user sources, uncertainty, review status, and a manual fallback. Assign data freshness, retention, incident response, and support ownership.

Prove quality

A release bar tied to the workflow

Test APIs and integrations; use a representative, versioned evaluation set with normal requests, missing answers, stale sources, permission negatives, and injection attempts. Evaluate retrieval, grounded answers, correct tool use, and abstention separately.

Compare the full human workflow against the baseline: handling time including review, critical-error rate, task completion, cost per completed request, and latency. Agree on numerical acceptance limits with the client before running the pilot.

Ship and adopt

A deployment is the start of operational ownership

Use CI/CD with tests, environment configuration, migration discipline, and rollback. Pilot with a small group, train a client champion, and keep existing workflows available. Log model/source versions and action traces with sensitive-data controls.

Review adoption, overrides, failures, and business outcomes with users. Hand over a runbook, access inventory, known limits, support terms, and a measured next-step recommendation.

AI development workflow: use Cursor, Claude Code, or your actual tools for exploration, implementation, review, tests, and documentation. Explain how you supply context, inspect diffs, verify execution, protect client data, and decide what is safe to automate. Faster code generation does not remove responsibility for correctness.

House-stack bridge: explain how your backend experience maps to ASP.NET Core APIs, dependency injection, workers, authentication, and data access; how your frontend experience maps to Angular components, services, forms, and testing. State your actual .NET/C# and Angular experience accurately, then describe a concrete ramp-up path in an existing client codebase.

Manage 2–3 engagements without making clients chase you

Keep one clear next decision per client

Track outcome, stage, owner, next milestone, client dependency, remaining budget, and next decision date. Reserve focused build time and explicit discovery/demo slots. Keep data, credentials, and AI contexts separated by client.

Prioritize by impact, commitment, and what unblocks others. If two commitments conflict, surface the options early and negotiate the sequence with the affected clients and technical lead.

A weekly update clients can act on

Outcome: what changed for users or what uncertainty was resolved.

Evidence: demo, pilot result, or completed decision.

Risk / decision: what needs attention, your recommendation, and the date needed.

Next: the next milestone, cost-to-complete range, and who owns each dependency.

Client-ready language: replace “the retrieval pipeline has low recall” with “the assistant is missing relevant policy documents, so some answers lack the evidence staff need. We're testing the search step on real requests before widening the pilot.” Then provide the technical details if asked.

Practice the questions aloud

Likely questions inferred from the posting, not a known interview script. Open each answer after trying a 60–90 second response. These are suggested approaches; add real evidence when asked about your experience.

1. A lead gives you two sentences and asks for an AI app. What do you do?

“I'd clarify the business outcome, then walk through a real example of the current process. I'd establish volume, effort, failure cost, users, data access, and who can make a decision. I would summarize what we know and what is still an assumption, then recommend an engagement that reduces the important uncertainty. I would avoid committing to an implementation before those facts support it.”

Follow-up: name the three questions you would ask first and the specific deliverable you would propose next.

2. When would you recommend an audit or advisory engagement instead of software?

“If the evidence is missing, I'd propose a bounded audit to establish the workflow, economics, data readiness, and ranked opportunities. If the facts are already clear and the client can execute, advisory may be enough. I would define a useful decision as the deliverable, with acceptance criteria and a decision owner. Neither needs to become a build to be successful.”

3. How do you push back without losing the client's trust?

“I acknowledge the outcome they want, explain the concern in terms of their cost or risk, and show an alternative. I'd say, ‘We can build that, but first let's test whether it addresses the delay we observed.’ Then I'd propose a small comparison and a decision date. The client should understand my reasoning and retain an informed choice.”

4. How do you estimate work when the scope is unclear?

“I'd separate discovery from implementation. I can price a bounded investigation around specific questions. For implementation, I would break the work into milestones, state dependencies and exclusions, provide a range, and identify what drives that range. Fixed commitments require a sufficiently clear boundary; changes need an explicit cost and schedule decision.”

5. How would you explain ROI to a nontechnical client?

“I'd start with the current workflow and calculate the difference we can actually measure: time, errors, throughput, or revenue. I'd include review effort, adoption, and ongoing operating costs, then show a base and a conservative case. If the benefit is capacity rather than reduced spending, I'd say that clearly. A pilot should validate the largest assumption before a larger commitment.”

6. Tell me about AI you shipped into a live product.

Use a real example: “The users were [who], and the AI performed [specific product capability]. I personally owned [scope]. We evaluated it against [baseline and test set], controlled [data/tool boundary], and rolled out through [process]. We observed [verified outcome]. One failure was [actual issue], and we changed [mechanism and recurrence check].”

Be ready to draw the architecture and explain what happened when the model was wrong. A story about generating code with an AI tool answers a different question.

7. How do you choose between RAG, an agent, and ordinary automation?

“I choose the simplest mechanism that passes the workflow's acceptance criteria. Structured, repeatable operations often need rules. Current private knowledge may need retrieval with permission checks and citations. Variable multi-step tasks may justify tool selection, but only with bounded actions, budgets, and recoverable state. I'd compare candidates on the same real cases before increasing complexity.”

8. How do you use AI development tools while staying accountable?

“I use AI across discovery, code, tests, review, and documentation, but I still own the result. I give it a bounded task and relevant context, review the changes, execute tests, verify integration behavior, and inspect risky paths directly. I follow the client's rules for source code and data. I can explain the system without relying on the assistant that helped build it.”

Add your actual tools and a specific example of a mistake your verification caught.

9. How would you work in .NET and Angular if your strongest stack is different?

“My strongest production stack is [actual stack]. The transferable work is API design, data modeling, authorization, UI state, testing, deployment, and operations. I'd start by tracing an existing request end to end, using the team's conventions, and delivering a small vertical slice with review. I'd be explicit about what I already know and where I need ramp-up rather than claiming experience I don't have.”

10. How do you keep three engagements moving?

“I keep a visible next milestone and next decision for each client, with budget, risks, and dependencies. I reserve focused delivery blocks and regular client touchpoints. I surface conflicts before a deadline is missed and bring a recommendation about sequencing. Experienced peers are thought partners for hard decisions, while I remain accountable for the engagement.”

11. The pilot works, but nobody uses it. What do you do?

“I'd observe users doing the work and look at where they abandon or override the tool. The issue may be trust, extra steps, missing integrations, or a mismatch with the real job. I'd fix the workflow with a client champion, train users, and measure end-to-end outcomes. If the tool still does not improve the job, I'd recommend stopping or changing direction.”

12. What if you ship a mistake or the client changes priorities?

“I would contain the impact, explain what is known and unknown, and give the client a clear next update. For a production incident, I'd use rollback or the manual path and investigate the cause with a recurrence check. For changed priorities, I'd restate the goal and present scope, cost, and timing options. I would not quietly absorb new work and let the original commitment fail.”

Questions to ask Cider

Engagements and authority

  • What proportion of engagements become builds, implementations, advisory work, or paid audits?
  • Who decides the engagement type and owns the proposal, pricing, and scope changes?
  • What distinguishes a successful first 90 days in this role?

Delivery and support

  • What does “2–3 engagements” mean in typical weekly effort and overlap?
  • How do engineers get design/code review and a second opinion on commercial or technical risk?
  • Who supports a system after launch, and how is adoption measured?

AI and stack

  • Which production AI use cases have delivered the clearest client value?
  • Which model, client-data, security, and evaluation standards are already established?
  • How often does a project require .NET/Angular versus choosing a stack around the client's needs?

Working arrangement

  • What New York overlap hours and client-meeting availability are expected?
  • For W-2 versus 1099, how do benefits, equipment, guaranteed hours, and nonbillable discovery time differ?
  • How are hours, payment terms, and after-launch availability agreed?

Rate discussion: connect your rate to the scope you can own and evidence of relevant delivery. Confirm the arrangement and expected hours before comparing compensation. Treat the posting's range as a conversation boundary, not a promise of a specific offer.

Three points to land

You built Papercusp from nothing: back-end services, data, a release pipeline, developer tooling, and observability. That's the same set of work this posting describes. Heron explicitly values "standing up platforms from greenfield."

01 · The company

Heron Power, in a minute

Transformers and converters are a bottleneck on the grid: traditional transformers are slow to make and depend on specialty steel that's in short supply. Heron is building solid-state transformers made of power electronics, which are smaller and more controllable, starting with converters that connect large solar, wind, and storage sites (and large power users such as data centers) to the grid. Their first product is Heron Link.

Key facts

  • Founded 2024–25 by Drew Baglino after 18 years at Tesla, where he ran batteries, motors, drive units, power electronics, and energy products.
  • Raised a $38M Series A in May 2025 and $140M in February 2026. Investors include Andreessen Horowitz and Breakthrough Energy. Tesla co-founder JB Straubel and former Tesla CFO Zach Kirkhorn are advisors.
  • Says it has about 50 GW of orders lined up with more than a dozen prospective customers. That figure is Heron's own claim.
  • A pilot with RWE at a battery site on a Texas wind farm. First installations are targeted for early 2027.
  • Engineering is based at a test lab in Scotts Valley, CA. Heron reported about 43 employees in 2025; it's larger now.

What the software team builds

  • The "Build" platform that runs the factory floor and supply chain. This is a manufacturing execution system (MES): it tracks every unit, part, test, and station.
  • Internal tools, back-end services, and data pipelines for engineers and operators, such as test data from hardware validation.
  • Infrastructure: AWS with infrastructure as code (Terraform or Pulumi), Kubernetes, GitHub Actions, Grafana, Prometheus.
  • Security and data integrity: access control, secrets, least privilege.

Embedded firmware for the converters is a separate team with its own openings.

The Tesla culture signal: the posting stresses "first principles," "minimal direction," and knowing "what to build, what to buy, and what to leave alone." Expect direct questions. Answer concisely, give numbers, and show that you decide and then ship.

05 · Answers to rehearse

Questions Ed is likely to ask

"Walk me through a system you designed."

Choose Papercusp's release pipeline. The problem: many agents commit to one tree, and broken code must not reach production. The design: an automatic process commits the work, a test gate checks a candidate, and main only moves forward when the gate is green; deploys roll back automatically. The trade-off: when the gate went red, a new candidate kept pulling in more breakage, so you froze the failing candidate and added fixes on top until it converged. The result: main stays green while about a hundred agents work. That example covers CI/CD, reliability, and judgment.

"How would you design the factory platform (MES) from scratch?"
  1. Data model first: parts, lots, serialized units, stations, routings (the sequence of steps), test results, and genealogy (which parts went into which unit).
  2. Event log: each station emits events as a unit moves through it. State is derived from the events, which gives you traceability and auditability.
  3. Offline tolerance: stations must keep working if the network or cloud is down, then sync safely later, with idempotent writes so nothing is recorded twice.
  4. Buy versus build: buy the ERP and procurement systems; build the parts that are unique to Heron, such as test-data pipelines and converter genealogy.
  5. Telemetry: high-volume test data goes into time-series or object storage, with summaries in Postgres.

Ask what they've already bought or built before you go deep.

"When do you build versus buy?"

Buy when the problem is common and not what differentiates you, such as ERP, accounting, identity, or monitoring. Build when it's core to how Heron works, or when vendor tools would force a factory process that doesn't fit. Count the full cost of integration and maintenance, not just the license. Example: DBOS for durable workflows. You used an existing library instead of writing your own, and focused your effort on the coordination layer.

"You'd be on-site in Scotts Valley. Does that work?"

Answer honestly: where you live now and whether you'd relocate or commute. Then ask what in-person schedule the software team actually keeps.

"What are your salary expectations?"

"I'm aligned with the posted range of $180K to $240K, and equity matters to me at this stage. I'd like to understand the level, Staff or Senior Staff, before giving a specific number." If pressed, name a number near the top of the range.

"Why the AI-agent work, and why come here now?"

Papercusp taught you how to make a small team move very fast without breaking things: tests, release gates, locking, and observability. That's what a hardware startup's software team needs. You can also bring AI-assisted development to the team, with the same guardrails.

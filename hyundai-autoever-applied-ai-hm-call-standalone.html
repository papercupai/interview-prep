What this call is, and is not

An hour with the hiring manager is long for a first conversation—expect a real technical discussion, not a résumé walk. It is still a conversation: no shared editor, no whiteboard unless they offer one. Depth is demonstrated by specificity, and the fastest way to lose a manager is a framework list where a story with a measured outcome belonged. Glassdoor rates Hyundai AutoEver interviews as moderate difficulty (2.5/5) with roughly a month from first contact to decision; treat that as weak signal, not a format.

HAEA's stated process haeaus.com/careers

  1. Recruiter conversation — “a call to discuss the role.” Done.

Sixty seconds that earn the technical conversation

Draft — every claim below is checkable in the code

I build the infrastructure that makes AI systems dependable in production rather than just impressive in a demo. Most recently that has been a platform for running about a hundred AI coding agents on one codebase: the agents talk to it over the Model Context Protocol—I built the server side, roughly 650 tools for coordination, work tracking, locks, scheduling, and release—on Postgres with pgvector for retrieval, a React operator UI, and a background engine that runs the release gate and deploys green several times an hour. Then prove it

06 · The four gaps

Say the gap first, then say the thing that is better than the gap

The rule

A hiring manager who lists five agent frameworks has met candidates who claim all five. The candidate who says “I have not shipped LangGraph—here is what I built instead, and here is how it maps” is more credible than four of those five, and more hireable than the one who fakes it and gets probed. Never bluff a named tool. Name the gap in one clause, pivot in the next.

Gap 1 — “Hands-on expertise with AutoGen, LangGraph, LlamaIndex, CrewAI, n8n”

The script

I have not shipped on LangGraph or CrewAI. What I built is the layer they abstract—pull-based work claims, per-file locks, a presence oracle, a supervisor that assigns and wakes workers—from Postgres primitives, because our constraint was a hundred agents on one branch and no framework handled that. I know the LangGraph model well enough to map onto it: their StateGraph is our plan, their checkpointer is our work-item checkpoint, their interrupt() is our owner-gated wall. The difference is where control flow lives—in an authored graph versus in shared state—and I can tell you what each buys. If your team is on LangGraph, I would be productive in it in days, and I would bring an opinion about where its execution model gets in the way.

Two evenings of study — the vocabulary you must own

  • LangGraph: StateGraph, typed state with reducers, conditional edges, Command, the Send API for fan-out, checkpointers and threads, interrupt() and the replay-before-interrupt caveat, LangGraph Platform vs. the library.
  • AutoGen: ConversableAgent, GroupChat and speaker selection, the 0.4 actor-model rewrite.
  • CrewAI: Agents, Tasks, Crews, sequential vs. hierarchical process, Flows.
  • LlamaIndex: ingestion pipeline, nodes, indices, query engines vs. chat engines, the retriever/postprocessor/synthesizer split.
  • n8n: a low-code workflow tool with an AI-agent node—know it is a workflow builder, not an orchestration framework, and that its presence suggests business users build some automations themselves.
Gap 2 — “TensorFlow or PyTorch, the Hugging Face ecosystem”

The script

My deep-learning work has been at the serving and embedding layer, not the training loop—I run Hugging Face-distributed encoders locally and I have had to reason hard about embedding spaces, dimension truncation and renormalization. I have not trained a PyTorch model for production. For the predictive-model side of this role I would reach for gradient-boosted trees first anyway—that is what I shipped—and I would want to see a real case where a neural model beats that baseline before adding the operational cost. If you have one, I would like to hear what it is.

Study before the call

  • The PyTorch training loop: nn.Module, Dataset/DataLoader, zero_grad → backward → step, .train() vs .eval(), torch.no_grad().
  • Hugging Face: AutoTokenizer/AutoModel, pipeline(), the Trainer API, PEFT/LoRA at the level of “what it is and when.”
  • Be ready to say why you would fine-tune an embedding model before fine-tuning a generator, for a RAG problem.
Gap 3 — “AWS SageMaker/Bedrock, Azure OpenAI, or Azure AI Foundry, along with MLOps”

The script

My cloud work has been on GCP—reproducible GCE provisioning, and a release pipeline that certifies a shared branch and deploys green several times an hour with automatic rollback. I have not built on Bedrock or Azure OpenAI. I understand the shapes—Bedrock as a model gateway with Knowledge Bases and Agents, Azure OpenAI as the enterprise-tenanted OpenAI endpoint, Foundry as the studio around it—and the MLOps concerns are the same ones I have already solved: model artifacts as versioned files, preflight before launch, capacity as a measured signal rather than an assumed one. Which of the two do you actually run? The posting names both.

Study before the call

  • Bedrock: the Converse API, Knowledge Bases (managed RAG over S3 + a vector store), Bedrock Agents, Guardrails, model access per region.
  • SageMaker: training jobs, endpoints, Model Registry, Pipelines—enough to say which piece replaces which part of your storewolf sidecar.
  • Azure OpenAI / AI Foundry: deployments-as-resources, content filters, the Assistants/Agent Service, prompt flow for evaluation.
  • Pick one and read its quickstart end to end so you can describe a concrete flow, not a product page.
Gap 4 — “Docker, and Kubernetes”

The script

Docker and compose, extensively—a dozen images across the platform. Not Kubernetes: the system was single-instance by design, and Kubernetes would have been ceremony without a workload to justify it. I know the objects and I know what I would need it for here—horizontal scaling of an inference-facing API, rolling deploys, per-tenant isolation. If the team runs on EKS or AKS I would expect to be shipping into it within the first weeks, and I would want to pair with whoever owns the cluster once rather than guess.

07 · Likely questions

What a hiring manager asks in an hour, and the shape of the answer

1. “Walk me through an AI system you took from prototype to production.”

Answer spine — Answer Bank N1, compressed to two minutes

  • The problem: many AI agents on one codebase, and making what they do verifiable.
  • The shape: agents as MCP clients, the platform as the server (~650 tools), Postgres underneath, a background engine for sweep-commit, gate, and deploy.
  • The scale, honestly: ~100 agents, 15,271 TS files, 7,786 test files, 920 migrations.
  • What is hard: every rule code review would enforce has to be enforced by the system. Then pick one rule and its incident.

Expect: “Who uses it?” Say the truth—one operator, alpha, no external users—and that the design target is a team that wants AI-assisted changes to enter a real pipeline with an audit trail. That is exactly HAEA's governance problem, so say so.

2. “Tell me about a RAG system you built. How did you make it accurate?”

Answer spine — the embedding-space incident

  • Hybrid retrieval: lexical (tsvector with a document-frequency term selector) fused with semantic (pgvector, HNSW cosine) via RRF.
  • Three embedding spaces at the same dimension. A preference flip changed the query embedder but not the stored vectors; nothing errored because both were 384-D.
  • Measured: searching for a row by its own exact text ranked it #6,574 of 8,383. Search silently returned noise.
  • The fix: record which embedder produced each vector, give each space its own table, and make the backfill revisit rows rather than only filling nulls. And the rule: the space is part of the schema, not a runtime preference.

Expect: “Why Postgres instead of a vector database?” One store, transactional with the data it indexes, no second system to keep consistent, HNSW is good enough at tens of thousands of rows. Name the ceiling honestly: at tens of millions of vectors or multi-region you would evaluate a dedicated store—and you would choose it by its filtering and freshness semantics, not its benchmark.

3. “How do you evaluate whether an LLM feature is actually good enough to ship?”

Answer spine — Answer Bank Q8

  • The failure mode is not wrong answers, it is confident plausible ones—so “done” must be substantiated, not asserted.
  • Completion requires evidence; a graded rubric against what was built; the grader cannot be the implementer, enforced structurally.
  • Kinds of evidence are distinguished: a citation to code or a test counts (content hash stored); a citation to a document does not.
  • Benchmarks as the probe answer: 141 scenario tests across ten roles; a pre-registered SWE-bench Pro subset so the goalposts cannot move after the run.

Bridge to their world: for a dealer-facing or owner-facing assistant, name the release rule—a frozen eval set, a hallucination rate with a threshold, a red-team set that must fail closed, and a named owner for the go/no-go. “We monitor it” is not a gate.

4. “When do you use multiple agents versus one? What orchestration pattern?”

Answer spine

  • Multiple agents when the work is decomposable and the pieces need isolation—separate context, separate failure domains, separate ownership. Not because it demos well.
  • Name the patterns and where you sit: authored state machines (LangGraph) for known workflows; supervisor/worker for decomposition; a blackboard—shared state, pull-based claims—for open-ended work, which is what you built and why.
  • The cost you pay: coordination overhead, no shared context between workers, and every rule must be enforced by the system because there is no reviewer at that volume.
  • The rule of thumb for an enterprise team: start with one agent and deterministic tools; add a second agent only when you can name the invariant that isolation buys.

Expect: the follow-up on LangGraph specifically. Use the Gap 1 script; do not improvise a claim of experience.

5. “How do you handle hallucination in a customer-facing application?”

Answer spine

  • Separate what the model may say from what the system may do. Anything with consequence—a vehicle command, a warranty determination, a price—goes through a deterministic validator that holds veto power. The model proposes; rules dispose.
  • Grounding: retrieval with provenance, answers that cite, and refusal as a first-class outcome with a measured refusal rate.
  • Bound the surface: tool results capped and projected so the model cannot be handed more than it can use—your ~1,500-token cap and projection arguments.
  • Provenance on inputs: your ledger stamping every prompt as human-typed or machine-injected exists because a summarized note was once promoted into a fabricated instruction. Prompt injection in a document-RAG system is the same failure class.

Automotive specificity: a wrong answer about a recall, a tow-rating, or a Bluelink remote command is a safety or legal event, not a chat error. Say that unprompted and the manager will hear that you understand why governance is 15% of the posting.

6. “How do you think about cost and latency for LLM features?”

Answer spine — Answer Bank N8, compressed

  • Capacity is a measured signal, not an assumed one: a probe reads authoritative limit headers on successful responses, not just 429s.
  • Admission is AIMD over measured headroom; routing prefers the same account each turn so the prompt cache stays warm; preflight one canary before launching a fleet.
  • The bug worth telling: two stores of the same truth—a database projection and an in-memory exclusion map—disagreed, and admission sat pinned at four while the controller allowed twenty-four. One action now converges both.
  • Cost per call: bound results at the source, cache stable prefixes, and pace around the cache window instead of waking to keep it warm.

Do not say “use a smaller model.” Say where the budget goes—retrieval, model, tools, rendering—and what degrades first when demand exceeds capacity.

7. “Tell me about a time you were wrong.”

Answer spine — Answer Bank Q6, and it is your best answer

  • Reported a relevance score of 0.03 against a 0.45 threshold as a 99.9% regression. The column stored two different quantities on two code paths; on one the maximum was 0.033. Two scales, one number.
  • The pattern: asserting from a single artifact without reading the thing that wrote it. Three times in one day.
  • The rule: before you report a number, find the code that writes it. The tell: the more damning a finding looks, the likelier you are misreading it.
  • The retraction—immediate, in the written record, because an unretracted finding keeps directing other people's work.

Why it works here: a captive IT org lives on trust between engineers and business owners. A candidate who has publicly retracted a wrong finding and changed process because of it is safer to hire than one with a clean record.

8. “How do you work with non-technical stakeholders? With teams in other time zones?”

Answer spine — Answer Bank Q13, the honest reframe

  • Be straight: much of your recent work has been asynchronous, coordinating automated systems rather than a room of engineers—and it forced habits that transfer.
  • Writing for someone who cannot ask a clarifying question: state scope, constraints, and what you have already ruled out, up front.
  • Hand reviewers evidence, not conclusions: “here is the failing case before and after” lets them check you.
  • A handoff is done when pickup is verified, not when it is sent.

The HAEA-specific angle: the parent company is in Seoul and the customers are sister companies. Ask how requirements arrive and how decisions get ratified across that seam. Asking is a better signal than claiming experience you do not have.

9. “Why are you looking? What do you want in your next role?”

Answer spine

  • You have spent a long stretch building a platform with one user—yourself—and the next step is a system with real users, real governance, and consequences for being wrong.
  • You want the whole loop: the model, the retrieval, the service, the UI, and the conversation with the person who has to trust it.
  • Do not badmouth anything. Do not mention compensation as a driver.

If they ask about the solo-project risk directly: answer with the Q13 reframe and the evidence-not-conclusions habit, then ask what collaboration looks like on their team so you can speak to it specifically.

10. “What would you do in your first ninety days?”

Answer spine

  • Days 1–30: learn what is in production, who the users are, what the governance path looks like, and which cloud you are actually on. Ship one small thing through the whole path to learn where it snags.
  • Days 31–60: instrument before changing. Put an eval set and a release rule under whatever LLM feature matters most.
  • Days 61–90: own one PoC-to-production promotion end to end, and write down the path so the next one is faster.

Signal: “instrument before customizing” and “ship one small thing through the whole path first” are what a manager wants to hear from an applied engineer. Architecture opinions come after.

09 · Questions for them

Ask three. Each one should tell you what the job actually is.

  1. Ask first
    “What is in production today versus in PoC—and what was the last thing that made the jump?” The single most informative question. Their answer tells you the maturity of the practice and whether “production-ready” in the posting is aspiration or fact.
  2. Ask second
    “The posting names both AWS Bedrock and Azure OpenAI. Which do you actually run, and is that a choice or a transition?” Reveals the platform, the politics, and how much of your first month is spent on which quickstart.
  3. Ask third
    “Who are the users—dealers, vehicle owners, employees at the affiliates—and who signs off before something reaches them?” Governance is 15% of the posting; this makes them describe it.
  4. “How do requirements arrive from the affiliates and from Seoul, and how does a technical disagreement get ratified?” The captive-IT question. Asking it shows you understand the org.
  1. “How is the team composed—ML engineers, full-stack, data—and who owns the cloud infrastructure this would run on?” Tells you whether “full-stack fluency” means occasional or daily.
  2. “What is the evaluation bar an LLM feature has to clear, and is it written down?” You have a worked answer ready if they turn it around.
  3. “Does this build on H Chat and the AI API Hub coming out of Seoul, or beside them for the US affiliates?” Names the parent's platform without being asked. Their answer tells you whether you inherit a runtime or build one—and how much of the MCP work is integration versus invention.
  4. “What does the security review for an LLM feature look like now, and who runs it?” Ask it plainly; do not mention the breach unless they do. It treats governance as engineering, and after 2025 they will have a real answer.
  5. “Why is the role open?” Growth, backfill, or a stalled initiative—each is a different job. Two sibling AI reqs (10214, 10783) are open at once, so “we are standing up the practice” is the likely answer—ask what the practice is meant to own in a year.
  6. Close with
    “Six months in, what would make you say this hire went really well?” A request for the success criteria; restate them back and you have closed the loop.

Never ask

“What's the culture like,” anything answerable from the careers page, or anything about compensation, remote policy, or PTO on the hiring-manager call. Those belong with the recruiter.

11 · The 60-minute clock

Budget the hour so the last ten minutes are yours

  1. 0:00 – 0:05 · Rapport and their framing

    Let them describe the team and the role first. Listen for which of the four inferred surfaces in section 02 they name—it re-targets everything after.

  2. 0:05 – 0:10 · Your opener

    Sixty seconds, then stop. If they ask a follow-up, you have earned the technical conversation.

  3. 0:10 – 0:35 · Their questions

    Two or three deep stories. Lead with MCP/agent interfaces (N6), have the embedding-space incident and the evaluation story ready. Ninety seconds each, then let them pull the thread.

  4. 0:35 – 0:48 · Technical discussion

    This is where the gaps surface. Use the section 06 scripts verbatim: name the gap, pivot to the thing you built, ask which tool they run.

  5. 0:48 – 0:57 · Your questions

    Three from section 09—production vs. PoC, which cloud, who signs off. Close with the six-month question.

  6. 0:57 – 1:00 · Close

    Restate their success criteria back in one sentence. Ask what the next step is and who owns it. Thank them; stop talking.

If the hour runs short

Cut the technical-discussion block, never your questions. A manager who did not get to ask everything will schedule a follow-up; a candidate who asked nothing does not get one.

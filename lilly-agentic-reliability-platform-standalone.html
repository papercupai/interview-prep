01 · The thesis

What they are actually buying

Read the JD's own words: the Reliability Engineering team is "in active transition from human-executed operations to engineering-led, agent-assisted reliability." That sentence is the whole job. They have an ops team doing repetitive work by hand, and they want someone to build the machine that makes most of that work disappear — then prove it disappeared.

"Deflection" is a service-desk term, and the supporting skills give the shape away: ServiceNow integration, Microsoft Teams intake. So the pipeline is concretely:

intake (ServiceNow ticket / Teams message) → classify (what kind of problem is this?) → act (run the remediation) → verify (did it actually work?) → escalate (hand to a human when confidence is low).

Deflection rate = the share of intake that reaches a correct terminal state without a human touching it.

Two structural observations worth saying out loud in the interview, because they show you have built one of these rather than read about them:

The verify step is where these platforms actually fail

Everyone builds intake, classification and action. Far fewer build honest verification, so the agent reports success and the ticket reopens two hours later. Insist that "resolved" means observed resolved — the alert cleared, the probe passed, the metric returned to band — not "the remediation script exited zero." A platform that cannot distinguish those two is reporting deflection it has not earned.

They ask for a data contract, which is the interesting half

The JD asks you to define "the data contracts that let the platform consume durable fixes from upstream engineering teams." Translation: deflection is a treadmill if the underlying defects are never fixed. The platform should be producing a ranked feed of recurring root causes back to product teams, and the JD's line about influencing "engineering leaders to adopt automation-friendly patterns at the source, not just downstream" is the same idea. Say plainly: the goal is not to automate toil forever, it is to make toil extinct — automation buys time for the durable fix, it does not replace it. That reframing lands well with reliability leaders and is genuinely correct.

Use this line. "A deflection number that goes up while the same incident class keeps recurring is a warning sign, not a win. I'd instrument both: deflection and recurrence, and treat a rising deflection rate on a flat recurrence curve as a signal that we're papering over something upstream."

02 · Your opener

Ninety seconds, no numbers you have not re-checked

Structure: what you build → the one hard thing you learned → why this role specifically. Do not recite a CV; they have it.

"I build agentic platforms — the runtime, not just the prompts. Most recently a multi-agent system where the interesting problems were all the ones people don't anticipate: how agents coordinate without clobbering each other's work, how you hand off to a human at exactly the right confidence threshold, and how you evaluate a system whose output is different every time you run it.

The lesson I keep coming back to is that non-determinism doesn't excuse you from reliability engineering — it raises the bar. An agent that's right 95% of the time is a fundamentally different operational object than a script that's right 100% of the time, and it needs its own SLOs, its own rollback story, and above all its own honest evaluation. The failure mode I watch for is an eval suite that passes whether or not the agent is working.

What draws me here is that this role owns both halves — the Kubernetes and Terraform underneath and the agent runtime on top. I've found you can't do the second well without controlling the first, and in a regulated environment where every automated action has to be auditable, that's even more true."

Adapt the first sentence to your real evidence. Everything above is a frame, not a claim about your history. Before you use any specific — a system, a number, a role — confirm you can defend it under follow-up. A confident specific you cannot substantiate is worse than a modest one you can.

04 · Architecture

The resolution platform, end to end

Expect to whiteboard this. Have a default design you can draw in two minutes and defend for twenty.

Confidence-thresholded handoff — get the asymmetry right

The JD names this explicitly. The naive version is a single confidence score with one cutoff. The version that shows judgment accounts for asymmetric cost:

In a pharma production estate, a wrong automated action is far more expensive than a missed deflection. A missed deflection costs a human twenty minutes. A wrong action on a GxP-relevant system can mean a deviation, an investigation, and a regulatory paper trail.

So the threshold is not tuned to maximize deflection. It is tuned for precision on the action path, with deflection maximized only subject to a false-action ceiling. Those are different optimization problems and conflating them is how these platforms get rolled back after one bad week.

Say that, and add the refinement: confidence should be per-action-class, not global. "Restart a stateless pod in a dev namespace" and "modify a record in a validated system" do not deserve the same bar. Tier the action catalog by blast radius, and let each tier carry its own threshold and its own approval requirement.

Agent state and memory — the question behind the question

When they ask about "agent state and memory," they are usually probing whether you understand that different state has different durability needs. A crisp answer:

Kind of stateWhere it belongsWhy
Workflow state — what is this agent doing, what step is it onDurable store (Postgres), not memoryMust survive a crash mid-incident. An agent that forgets it already restarted a service will restart it again.
Structured facts — this host is in this cluster, this ticket maps to this serviceRelational, queryableCode branches on it. Absence must be an answer you can act on.
Fuzzy knowledge — how this class of incident usually behavesVector / semantic retrievalValue is associative; a miss costs a rediscovery, not a wrong action.

The load-bearing principle, and a genuinely good thing to say aloud:

"Ask what a miss means." A relational store returning zero rows is authoritative absence — you can branch on it. A semantic search returning nothing is uninterpretable: it might not exist, or the embedding just didn't match. So anything where the agent must distinguish "not there" from "not retrieved" cannot live behind semantic retrieval alone. Put a routing decision on that and you avoid a whole class of confidently-wrong agent behavior.

Idempotency and the replay problem

Underrated and very interview-worthy: agent actions must be idempotent, because agents retry. A webhook fires twice, a run resumes after a crash, a human re-triggers. Every action in the catalog needs an idempotency key and a safe-to-replay contract, and the ones that genuinely cannot be made idempotent should be flagged as requiring human confirmation regardless of confidence.

05 · Your differentiator

Evaluating systems that are different every run

The JD asks for "evaluation and guardrail frameworks for non-deterministic systems: automated evals, quality scoring, drift detection, and feedback loops." This is where most candidates get vague. Be specific and you separate yourself immediately.

The question almost nobody asks: does your eval actually fail?

An evaluation suite that has never failed is not a passing grade — it is an untested instrument. A test that passes whether or not the behavior is present is decoration, not coverage.

The fix is to deliberately break the thing and confirm the eval catches it. Mutate the agent's prompt, degrade the retrieval, swap in a deliberately-wrong tool response — and assert your eval goes red. If it stays green, your eval was measuring nothing and you would never have found out.

This has a name in software — mutation testing — and almost nobody applies it to LLM evals. Bringing it up signals you have been burned by a green dashboard that meant nothing, which is exactly the scar tissue they are hiring for.

The false-green family, generally

Related and worth having ready: most bad automated conclusions come from an instrument that measured nothing but reported a clean result. A test selector that matched zero tests and exited zero. A search whose output was truncated before the real hit. A query against the wrong table returning zero rows. In each case the empty result is indistinguishable from a genuine all-clear.

The discipline: never act on a negative result without a positive control. Before you believe "the agent found no matching incidents," confirm the same search returns something you know is there. In an agentic platform, this is what stops an agent from confidently reporting "nothing is wrong" because its detector was broken.

A layered eval stack you can sketch

LayerWhat it catchesRuns when
Golden dataset — frozen incidents with known-correct resolutionsRegression in classification or action selectionEvery CI run; blocks merge
Structured-output validationSchema drift, malformed tool callsEvery run, cheap, deterministic
LLM-as-judge with human calibrationQuality of reasoning and resolution write-upsNightly + pre-release; sampled
Shadow / canary against live trafficReal-world distribution shift the golden set missedContinuous on a traffic slice
Drift detection on inputs and outputsIncident mix changed; model provider changed under youContinuous, alerting

Have an opinion on LLM-as-judge. It is useful and it is not free: the judge is itself non-deterministic and drifts. Calibrate it against human labels periodically and track judge–human agreement as its own metric. A judge nobody has audited is a confident number with nothing behind it.

Drift you do not control

A sharp point for a regulated environment: when a model provider silently updates a model behind an endpoint, your validated system's behavior can change without any deployment on your side. That is a real problem for change control. The mitigation is pinned model versions, explicit promotion between versions, and evals gating that promotion — never floating aliases in production. Say this and you have demonstrated you understand why pharma treats AI differently.

06 · Knowledge

"Every resolved incident becomes training data"

The JD calls the knowledge base "a compounding deflection lever." Agree with the ambition, then show you know where it breaks.

The staleness problem is the whole problem

A knowledge base built from resolved incidents accumulates entries that were true when written. Services get rewritten; runbooks go stale. A retrieval system has no concept of "this is outdated" — it will happily surface a two-year-old resolution with high similarity and the agent will act on it.

Three things that actually help:

1. Every entry carries a lifetime. A claim about current system state gets an expiry; a durable principle does not. Forcing the author to declare which is a cheap, high-yield discipline.

2. Supersession must be a real operation. If "correcting" an entry means writing a second one, you now have two entries competing at retrieval and the wrong one can win. Ensure you can update in place and verify the update landed.

3. Feed outcomes back as signal. When a retrieved entry leads to a failed resolution, that is a labeled negative. Use it — demote the entry, flag it for review. Most knowledge bases only ever accumulate, never prune, and quality decays silently.

Hybrid retrieval, and when RAG is the wrong tool

They ask for "hybrid retrieval," meaning semantic + lexical. Have the reason ready: semantic search is bad at exact identifiers. Error codes, hostnames, ticket numbers, stack-trace symbols — these are precisely what appears in incidents, and embeddings blur them. Lexical (BM25) nails them. Fuse both, and reciprocal-rank fusion is the usual sane default.

And the maturity signal: sometimes retrieval is the wrong answer entirely. If the fact is structured and authoritative — which host runs which service, who owns this alert — that is a database query, not a similarity search. Reaching for RAG where a join would do is a common and expensive mistake.

07 · Substrate

Cloud-native, CI/CD, and observability for agents

What makes agentic CI/CD different

Ordinary pipelines test deterministic code. An agent pipeline has to gate on a statistical result, which changes the design:

  • Eval gates, not just test gates — the gate is "golden-set score ≥ threshold and no regression beyond noise," which means you need a handle on run-to-run variance before you can set a threshold at all.
  • Version the whole bundle — prompt, model version, tool schema, retrieval index. Changing the prompt alone changes behavior, so prompts are deployable artifacts under version control, not config someone edits live.
  • Canary on traffic slices with automatic rollback — measured on resolution quality and escalation rate, not just error rate and latency. An agent that silently starts escalating everything is "healthy" by infrastructure metrics and broken by every metric that matters.
  • Rollback must include the index — rolling back code while leaving a re-embedded retrieval index in place gives you a configuration nobody ever tested.

Observability: the agent-decision layer is the new part

Prometheus/Grafana/OpenTelemetry for the platform is table stakes. The JD's phrase "agent-decision telemetry" is the interesting ask. Concretely, every agent run should emit a traceable record of: what it was asked, what it retrieved, what it decided and with what confidence, which tools it called with what arguments, what it observed back, and why it escalated or did not.

Two reasons, and give both — the second is the one that resonates in pharma:

  1. Debugging. Without it, "the agent did something weird" is unfalsifiable.
  2. Audit. In a regulated environment you must be able to reconstruct, after the fact, exactly why an automated action was taken. That is not a nice-to-have; it is the difference between a platform that can touch validated systems and one that cannot.

SLOs for a non-deterministic system

Good answer: you need two tiers, and people conflate them.

TierExample SLIs
Platform health — classic, deterministicAPI availability, p99 latency, queue depth, inference error rate
Agent quality — statistical, needs windowsResolution correctness on sampled review, false-action rate, escalation precision, deflection rate, time-to-resolution

The second tier cannot be measured per-request in real time; it needs sampling windows and some human review. Saying that shows you have actually run one of these rather than designed one on a slide.

08 · The frame you may be missing

GxP, Part 11, and why pharma is different

If you have not worked in life sciences, this section is your highest-leverage hour of prep. You will not be expected to be a compliance expert. You will be expected to understand why the constraints exist — and a candidate who does stands out sharply against one who treats "regulated" as a synonym for "slow."

The vocabulary, briefly

TermWhat it means for you
GxPUmbrella for Good Manufacturing/Clinical/Laboratory Practice. A system is "GxP-relevant" if it touches product quality, patient safety, or data supporting a regulatory submission. Those systems carry far heavier change-control requirements.
21 CFR Part 11FDA rule on electronic records and electronic signatures. Practically: audit trails that are attributable, time-stamped, and non-modifiable; access controls; and the ability to demonstrate who did what and when.
Validation (CSV / CSA)Documented evidence that a system does what it is specified to do. The newer Computer Software Assurance guidance is explicitly more risk-based and friendlier to automated testing — worth knowing, because it is the lever that lets modern CI/CD coexist with validation.
ALCOA+Data integrity principles: Attributable, Legible, Contemporaneous, Original, Accurate (+ complete, consistent, enduring, available). A useful checklist for what your agent-decision telemetry must capture.

The connection to make

Here is the synthesis that makes you sound like you belong there:

"The audit trail a regulator wants and the observability an engineer wants are nearly the same artifact. Attributable, time-stamped, tamper-evident records of what the system did and why — that's Part 11, and it's also just good agent-decision telemetry. I'd build one and satisfy both, rather than bolting compliance on afterward.

The place I'd be careful is autonomy scope. I'd want a clear, enforced boundary between GxP-relevant systems and everything else, with the agent's action catalog tiered accordingly — full autonomy on low-blast-radius non-GxP work, human-in-the-loop as a hard requirement on anything validated, regardless of how confident the model is."

That last sentence matters. It tells them you will not have to be restrained — you already understand where the brakes belong, which is exactly the anxiety a reliability leader has about handing agents production access in pharma.

09 · Numbers

Talking deflection and toil credibly

The JD asks twice for measurable outcomes — "with the numbers to show it." Prepare one story with real figures you can defend. If you do not have a clean deflection number, do not invent one; use a toil-reduction or cycle-time number and say what it is.

Shape of a strong answer: baseline → intervention → result → what it cost → what you would do differently.

The last two are what make it believable. Anyone can claim a percentage. Someone who says "it also increased our false-escalation rate for the first month until we retuned the threshold, and I'd run a shadow period first next time" has obviously lived it.

Metrics to have opinions about

  • Deflection rate — define the denominator carefully. Deflection of eligible intake is honest; deflection of all intake is inflated by things that were never automatable.
  • False-action rate — the metric that protects the program's existence. Track it from day one, before anyone asks.
  • Escalation precision — when the agent hands to a human, was that the right call? Over-escalation burns the trust you need; under-escalation burns something worse.
  • Toil hours reclaimed — the number leadership actually feels. Translate deflection into engineer-hours returned.
  • Recurrence — see §01. The honesty metric.
  • Cost per resolution — inference is not free, and the JD lists FinOps familiarity. A deflection that costs more than the human it replaced is not a win.

If asked for a number you do not have: "I don't have a defensible figure for that and I'd rather not give you one I can't stand behind. What I can tell you precisely is [the thing you did measure]." Interviewers at this level read that as integrity, not weakness — and it is the same instinct they want in someone reporting deflection metrics to leadership.

10 · Leadership

Senior IC: influence without org-chart authority

The JD is unusually explicit: "Leads through what they build and how they write, not through org-chart authority" and "Scale agentic intelligence through systems, standards, and people — not individual heroics." Both are invitations to specific stories.

"Tell me about influencing a team that didn't report to you."

The strongest version involves changing someone's defaults rather than winning an argument — a golden-path template, a linter, a shared library that made the right thing easiest. The JD's phrase "automation-friendly patterns at the source" is exactly this: you do not nag teams into emitting better telemetry, you make the paved road emit it.

"How do you set an engineering bar?"

Avoid "I do thorough code reviews." Better: a bar is something that holds when you are not in the room, which means it is encoded — in CI checks, in templates, in review checklists, in written standards. Mention that you would rather write the check than repeat the comment.

"How would you mentor senior engineers in agentic AI?" likely

Note the word senior — these are experienced engineers new to a domain, not juniors. The honest answer is that experienced engineers mostly need the mental model shift: from deterministic to statistical thinking, from "does it work" to "how often and how does it fail." Pairing on a real eval suite teaches that faster than any doc.

"Disagreement with an architect on a pattern."

The JD says you partner with a senior architect. They are checking you can disagree productively and then commit. Have a real example where you changed your mind on evidence — that version is more convincing than one where you were right.

"Standing up a new capability from a small founding team"

Listed under what you should bring, and it reframes the role: this platform may not exist yet, or barely. Ask directly (see §11). If it is greenfield, your first-90-days answer should be unglamorous and sequenced: instrument and measure the current toil before automating any of it, pick one high-volume low-blast-radius incident class, prove the loop end to end including verification and audit, then expand the action catalog. Resist the pull to build the general platform first.

11 · Your questions

Ask things that change your decision

Pick four or five. The good ones are the ones whose answers would genuinely affect whether you want the job.

Scope and maturity

  • "Does the agentic resolution platform exist today, or am I standing it up? What's running in production right now, and what's the current deflection rate?"
  • "What's the split between the platform substrate and the agent layer in practice — is this genuinely one role, or two that were merged because the right person could do both?"
  • "How large is the production estate — applications, incident volume per week?"

The hard ones — these are the differentiators

  • "What's the blast-radius boundary today? Can an agent take an action on a GxP-relevant system, and if not, what would have to be true for it to?"
  • "Has the platform ever taken a wrong automated action in production? What happened, and what changed afterward?" — the single most informative question you can ask. The answer tells you their real maturity and their real culture.
  • "How does validation and change control actually work for a system whose behavior can shift when a model provider updates something? Has that come up yet?"
  • "The JD mentions data contracts for consuming durable fixes from upstream teams. Is that feedback loop live, or aspirational? Do upstream teams currently act on what reliability finds?"

The one you should not skip

"The posting mentions flexible hours including some weekends and holidays. Can you walk me through what that looks like in practice — is there a formal on-call rotation with compensation, or is it more ad hoc? How often did the team get paged outside hours last quarter?"

This is a reliability role supporting continuous operations, so some of this is legitimate and expected. But "flexible hours may be required" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and a team mid-transition from manual ops is exactly the situation where a small group absorbs a lot of unplanned nights. Ask it neutrally and listen for whether they have real numbers. Vagueness here is itself the answer.

Success and support

  • "What does success look like at six months, in numbers you'd actually track?"
  • "Who else is on the engineering side of this — team size and shape?"
  • "Which build-vs-buy calls are still open? The JD says I'd own them, so I'm curious what's genuinely undecided."

What you are actually walking into

That 5.0 minutes is almost certainly total answer time, not session length. Typical screens run 20–40 minutes across 3–5 questions; five minutes points to a very short front-door screen — most likely two questions, possibly three. Budget 20 minutes end-to-end with setup.

Q1 Near-certain 0:30 prep · ~2:00 answer

“I'm a software engineer who builds AI platforms — specifically the infrastructure that lets AI systems do real work safely at scale.

Q3 Possible — have it loaded 0:30 prep · ~2:00 answer

Describe a difficult technical problem you solved.

If the screen is only two questions, this won't appear — but it costs little to hold ready, and it converts directly to the next round.

situation → task → action → result + lesson

“The hardest problem I've solved recently was making many AI agents safe to run against one shared codebase at the same time.

Individually the agents worked fine. Together, they silently overwrote each other — two agents editing one file, and the second write erased the first with no error and no trace. The failure was invisible, which is what made it dangerous.

My task was to make concurrent work safe without collapsing everyone down to one-file-at-a-time.

What I did was enforce it at the tool layer instead of relying on convention. Every write goes through an arbitration step that claims a lock first; if another agent holds that file, the write is blocked and the agent is told who holds it and why, so it can pivot instead of just failing. I paired that with an append-only coordination log, so every claim and handoff is auditable afterward.

The result was that concurrent editing became safe by default — throughput went up, and that whole class of silent-overwrite bug disappeared. The lesson I took: if a rule matters, enforce it in the system — don't document it and hope.”

Section 04

Delivery — where the points actually go

On a recorded screen, delivery is scored as heavily as content, and it is far easier to fix. Most candidates lose here, not on substance.

The single biggest one

Look at the camera lens, not at your own image. Drag the recording window to the very top of the screen, directly beneath the webcam, so your eyeline sits close to the lens. Watching yourself reads on video as avoiding eye contact.

  • Light from in front. A window or lamp facing you. Never a window behind you — it turns you into a silhouette.
  • Camera at eye level. Raise the laptop on books. A low camera angle is unflattering on everyone.
  • Wired earbuds beat a laptop mic — laptop mics pick up room echo and fan noise.
  • Kill every interruption. Phone on Do Not Disturb, Slack quit, browser tabs closed — they cost bandwidth and can fire a notification sound mid-answer.
  • In the 30-second prep, write three words — not sentences. Reading an answer is instantly visible and lands flat.
  • Signpost out loud. “Two reasons — first… second…” Structure that is audible reads as organized thinking.
  • Land the plane. Stop cleanly when you're done. Finishing strong at 1:40 beats being cut off mid-sentence at 2:00. Never trail into “…so, yeah.”
  • Push your energy above normal. Video flattens affect noticeably. What feels slightly over-animated in the room reads as engaged on playback. Open with a smile.

Highest return on the list

Record one full timed rehearsal on your phone and watch it back once. It is uncomfortable and it is the single most useful thing you can do in the next two days. You will catch one specific habit — a filler word, a glance away, a fade at the end — and fixing that one thing is worth more than another hour of rewriting.

Section 07 · added 2 Sep 2026

Question bank — mapping Papercusp onto the standard questions

Everything above is tuned to a five-minute one-way screen. This section is for the rounds after it — live technical and behavioral, any employer.

The recipe — every question in this bank

The constraint → why the obvious approach fails → what you did → the guarantee.

Most candidates give beat three only. The constraint is what makes the answer yours; the guarantee — what you did so it can't silently come back — is what separates an engineer from someone who closed a ticket. Keep it under ninety seconds and let them ask.

Every number below is taken from the code, not from memory. That precision is the whole advantage — don't round it up. One inflated figure invites a probe, and every probe after it lands on softer ground.

Q · CI/CD Near-universal for platform roles ~2:00 answer

Walk me through your CI/CD pipeline. How does code get to production safely?

Most answers are a tool list — “Actions, run the tests, deploy on merge.” Forgettable. Talk about what your pipeline does when it is red; that is where the engineering is.

the constraint → why the obvious thing fails → freeze + inherit → the guarantee

“We have a constraint most teams don't: about a hundred AI agents commit into one shared branch, continuously. A gate has to certify that branch before it promotes and deploys.

The obvious thing when the gate fails is to fix it and re-run at the current tip. That doesn't converge here — every re-cut re-admits everything that landed in the meantime, so you import new breakage faster than you clear the old. It's a treadmill.

So we freeze instead. The first real red pins that exact commit as the candidate, and fixes land on top of it. Each repair run then re-runs only two things: the tests that are still failing, and the tests whose related files changed since the freeze. Anything that passed at freeze time and hasn't been touched is inherited, not re-executed.

The gate goes green when that delta is green and the broken list is empty — so we still certify the whole candidate, we just assemble the proof from a chain of verdicts instead of re-running the tree every hour.

And it fails safe in one direction only: anything we can't positively verify falls back to the full run. A silently narrowed run would hand you a false green, and that is the one outcome that has to be impossible.”

If there's room — the number that lands

“The reason we found it: the selector diffed against main, and main only moves when the gate is green. So a red branch froze the diff base and every run got bigger — at worst 4,572 changed paths against a base 3,868 commits behind, 105 test tasks, where the change that actually mattered was 23. Recovery cost scaled with the age of the outage, so it never self-healed.”

If they push

“How is narrowing the run sound?”
Promotion never narrows — only selection does. Each verdict is rendered against its predecessor's anchor, so the chain composes back to the last green pin. Inheritance, not omission.
“What if a fix breaks something that was passing?”
It gets re-selected. A fix is a file change, and that change's affected radius pulls in the tests covering it — that's the “related files changed” half doing the work.
“Couldn't a partial run certify code nobody tested?”
Any run carrying a scope flag measured less than its full radius and is barred from advancing the watermark. The subtlety: the flag has two spellings, space and equals, and a naive check misses the second — so we reproduce the CLI's own parsing instead of approximating it.
“Isn't the frozen candidate just stale code?”
The freeze is the verdict anchor, not the work — the head being repaired converges forward onto the live branch when it's safe.
“How do you stop two people fixing the same red?”
One queue, single ownership, a status cell naming the holder. Before that: 37 agents, 97 attempts, one failure.
Don't say

“We run tests on every commit and deploy when they pass.” True of everyone.

Do say

Lead with the constraint. “A hundred agents on one branch” is the sentence that makes them lean in and ask a question only you can answer.

Q · TestingVery likely

How do you decide what to test? How do you know your tests are actually catching anything?

Everyone answers “unit, integration, e2e, aim for coverage.” Coverage is an input metric. Answer the second half instead.

wrong metric →the real check →the silent-pass trap

“Coverage tells you a line executed, not that anything would have noticed if it broke. The question I actually care about is: has this test ever failed? A guard that has never failed is a guard you haven't tested.

So we made that checkable. There's a probe that deliberately breaks the thing under test and asserts the guard catches it. If the mutant survives, your guard is weaker than you think. It also refuses a mutation that changed nothing — a no-op mutation makes any test look strong.

The subtler version of the same problem is a suite that measures nothing and exits zero. We had commands selecting zero test files and reporting success, which is indistinguishable from passing. Now the runner refuses a run that matched no tests, and the typechecker refuses a run that compiled no files.

And one ordering rule: typecheck last. Our test runner strips types to execute, so a green suite carries zero type information — if you typecheck, then fix a failing test, your clean verdict predates your last edit.”

“Isn't mutation testing too slow to run?”
It's a targeted probe at authoring time, not a matrix — and it runs against a copy or a historical commit rather than the live tree, because our tree is auto-committed every few minutes and a mutant left in place can get committed even when nothing goes wrong.
“How do you know a new test is any good?”
Prove it can fail at the moment you write it. That's the only point where the answer is cheap.
Q · DebuggingNear-universal

Tell me about the hardest bug you've had to debug.

They want your diagnostic method, not the bug. Pick one where the instrument was wrong — it's a level above “I read the stack trace.”

the symptom →why it was hard →the class →the guard

“The hardest ones share a shape: the instrument lies, and it lies in a way that looks like a clean answer.

Concrete one — a liveness check reported a process was gone. It wasn't; it was running and burning a full core. The check used a flag that didn't exist on that build of the tool, so it exited with a usage error, and the shell turned that non-zero exit into “gone.” A well-formed answer to the question we asked, and completely false.

What makes that hard is there's no error to find. It's the same class as a search truncated before the match, a query against the wrong table returning zero rows, a test command that matched zero files and exited zero. Every one returns “nothing,” and “nothing” is indistinguishable from a real absence.

So the fix wasn't the flag — it was the rule: never act on an empty result from an instrument that can't prove it measured. Existence checks return counts or file lists, not truncated output. And that liveness check now observes the process from outside, because a saturated process stops emitting its own heartbeat while looking perfectly healthy from the inside.”

“How do you catch that class in general?”
Ask what a false negative would look like. If it's identical to success, you need a positive control — run the same check against something you know is there.
“What changed so it can't recur?”
Not just the call sites — the shapes that produce it are refused at the gate. Every incident is also a detector failure, so you fix the detector too.
Q · ConcurrencyLikely for platform roles

Tell me about a race condition or concurrency problem you've dealt with.

Extends Q3 above. Two examples beat one, if the second is small and sharp.

the race →why convention failed →enforcement →the second race

“Two of them, same lesson.

First: dozens of agents editing one shared codebase. Two edit the same file and the second write erases the first — no error, no trace. Invisible, which is what made it dangerous. We enforced it at the tool layer rather than by convention: every write claims a lock first, and a blocked writer is told who holds the file and why, so it can pivot instead of just failing.

Second, smaller and I like it more. Adding a database migration meant picking the next number by listing the directory and adding one — two agents doing that concurrently pick the same number. Now the number comes from an allocator that reserves it under a Postgres advisory lock, so there's no gap to race in. And the file is written with a suffix the migration runner ignores, so a half-written migration can't be picked up and executed while you're still editing it.

If a rule matters, enforce it in the system. A documented convention is the one that gets violated at 3am by whoever didn't read the doc.”

“Why not just serialize everything?”
Safe and useless. The point of per-file arbitration is that concurrent work stays concurrent — you only serialize the actual collision.
“What if a lock holder dies?”
TTL plus heartbeat. A lock that can't expire means one crashed process wedges everyone.
Q · DesignVery likely

Walk me through a design decision you're proud of. / How do you keep a large system from rotting?

Answer with a rule, not a component. It generalizes, which is what they're testing for.

the recurring bug →the reframe →the ladder →what made it stick

“The design I'm proudest of is a rule rather than a component.

We kept hitting the same bug in different costumes: a value in config or documentation that describes the code — a path, a flag name, an ‘is this enabled' boolean, a list of who emits an event. It's a second copy of a truth the code already owns, and it always drifts. Our worst instance: the event catalog listed which events had emitters, by hand. The single most-fired event in the whole system was recorded as unregistered — and fourteen subscribers were parked waiting on an event with no emitter anywhere.

So we wrote it as a ladder and you take the highest rung that fits. Derive it from the source. If you can't, pin it — the prose stays, but a build check fails when it diverges. If static analysis can't see it, attest it against a runtime ledger on a schedule. Only if none of those work do you hand-maintain it, and then you have to say why.

What made it stick is that ‘a new config field restating what the code already knows' became a review smell people name out loud. It stopped being a bug we fixed and became a category we recognize.”

“Isn't deriving everything expensive?”
That's why it's a ladder. Pinning is one test. The expensive thing is rediscovering the drift six months later.
“How do you know the derived value is right?”
It fails the build when it disagrees with the source — a much stronger property than being correct on the day someone wrote it.
Q · Being wrongHighest-value answer here

Tell me about a time you were wrong, or made a significant mistake.

Heavily weighted, and most candidates give a disguised brag. A real one with a real mechanism is disproportionately convincing — and you have one.

the mistake →the pattern →the rule →the retraction

“A real one — and it happened three times in a single day, which is what made me take it seriously.

I reported a metric as evidence of a serious regression: a relevance score sitting at 0.03 against a documented threshold of 0.45, which read as 99.9% of results falling below the floor. It was wrong. That column stores two different things depending on which code path wrote it, and on one of those paths the maximum possible value is 0.033. I was comparing numbers on two different scales. The same day I'd also called a feature unimplemented based on one search in one file — it was implemented in a different file — and told someone a finding hadn't been recorded when it had, as a comment I hadn't read.

Same pattern each time: I asserted a conclusion from a single artifact without reading the thing that produced it. So the rule I hold now is before you report a number, find the code that writes it — confirm the units and the scale first.

And the tell is counterintuitive: the more damning a finding looks, the more likely you're misreading it. A real regression rarely produces a perfectly clean 99.9%; a suspiciously round number is usually a definitional artifact.

The other half is retraction. I'd already told someone it was real, so that has to be corrected immediately and in the written record, not quietly dropped — a bad finding you don't retract keeps directing other people's work.”

“How do you apply that under time pressure?”
It's cheap — reading the writer is one file. The expensive path is the one where someone spends a day investigating a mechanism that doesn't exist.
“Did anything change beyond your own habit?”
Yes. The surfaces that carry a claim forward now require you to mark whether it's something you observed or something you inferred, so an unverified hypothesis can't quietly harden into a stated fact.
Q · ObservabilityLikely

How do you know when something is wrong before a user tells you?

Skip the tool names. Three specific properties beat “we use Datadog.”

provenance →deltas not snapshots →the self-report trap

“Three things I'd point at.

First, our process list carries provenance. Standard tools tell you what's running; ours tells you who launched it, for which piece of work, and what it costs. That's the difference between ‘something is using four cores' and ‘this task is, and here's who owns it.' It also means we terminate by task identity rather than by process name — killing by name pattern has twice taken down things nobody intended, and identity can't hit a recycled process ID.

Second, CPU usage is a delta, not a reading. A single sample gives you a lifetime average, so a process idle for an hour and saturated right now reads as idle. You sample twice and difference — and you measure the whole process tree, because the parent is usually at zero while a child burns a core.

Third, the one I'd emphasize: a health signal emitted by the thing being judged cannot detect that thing being saturated. A blocked event loop stops emitting its own heartbeat while looking perfectly asynchronous from the inside. If it matters, observe from outside the process.”

“What do you alert on?”
Transitions, not levels. A level tells you where you are; a transition tells you something changed, which is what a human can act on.
“How do you avoid alert fatigue?”
Anything that fires and self-recovers is still a bug — file it rather than silence it. A false alarm is a defect in the alarm.
Q · AI evaluationMost role-relevant

How do you evaluate whether an AI system is actually doing a good job?

For any AI-platform role this is the question that decides it. Almost everyone answers with benchmarks; answer with accountability.

the real failure mode →evidence →independence →what counts as proof

“The failure mode with AI output isn't wrong answers — it's confident, plausible ones. So the design goal is to make ‘done' a claim that has to be substantiated rather than asserted.

Three pieces. Completion requires evidence — what was tested, what the result was, how it was verified. A bare ‘done' gets reopened.

For anything substantial there's a graded rubric: a handful of outcome criteria written against what was actually built rather than against the plan, and every rating cites concrete evidence.

And the part I'd want to talk about — the grader can't be the implementer, enforced structurally rather than by policy. The system resolves who authored the work and refuses any grader in that author's lineage, including one they spawned themselves. So you can't launch your own grader; you have to route the work to somebody independent. That constraint is annoying in exactly the right way.

We also distinguish kinds of evidence: a citation to code or a test counts as verification, a citation to a document explicitly does not. It's very easy to prove you wrote about something instead of proving you built it.”

“Isn't that a lot of process?”
It's the difference between a demo and something you'd let touch production — and it's mostly automatic, because the refusals happen at the write rather than in review.
“What about subjective quality?”
That's what the rubric is for. You can't score taste objectively, but you can force the judgment to be explicit, evidenced and attributable.
Q · DisagreementStandard behavioral

Tell me about a technical disagreement and how you resolved it.

Don't tell a persuasion story — everyone does. Tell one about making the resolution stick.

the real problem →the reframe →the payoff

“What I learned here wasn't about persuasion — it's that most disagreements I thought I'd resolved came back, because the resolution lived in a chat message.

A message isn't addressable after it's delivered. If someone ends up with a wrong paraphrase of the decision, there's no way back to the source. So rulings that other people have to follow get recorded as decisions attached to the plan they govern, with the reasoning, at the moment they're made.

The payoff surprised me. Someone re-read a recorded decision, realised their own earlier work had been wrong on most of the places they'd touched, corrected it, and then swept up several more instances of the same mistake. A chat message cannot produce that outcome, because nobody re-reads a chat message. That's when I stopped treating it as bookkeeping.”

“What if you turn out to be the one who's wrong?”
The mechanism works better in that direction — a written decision is falsifiable, and superseding it is a normal operation instead of an argument.
Q · SecurityLikely at a defense prime

How do you think about security in a system like that?

the threat model →one enforced seam →the flag discipline

“The threat model is unusual, because the untrusted actor is partly the system itself. Automated agents with real write access — so the question isn't only ‘can an attacker get in' but ‘what can a well-intentioned actor destroy by accident.'

The answer is one enforced seam. Every write goes through a single path that runs the guards — lock arbitration, secret detection, protection on generated and migration files. The important consequence is that any route around that path is itself the vulnerability: editing a file with a shell command instead of the tool bypasses every guard at once, silently. So that's blocked, not discouraged.

For dangerous capabilities we use flags, with one rule I think is the real insight. Default-off is only for things that are genuinely unsafe — irreversible migrations, anything reaching outside the system, privilege escalation, kill switches. Everything else ships on, because a parking lot for ‘finished but scary' work is where things go to never be verified. The off-by-default list is size-capped and can only shrink, so shipping a new one means graduating an old one.”

“Where do secrets live?”
Never in the tree — injected config outside the repo, and the write path detects and refuses them.
“How would you audit what the system did?”
An append-only record of every claim, decision and completion with evidence attached. That's also what makes it reviewable rather than merely functional — which is the whole argument for this kind of platform in a regulated environment.
Q · PerformancePossible

Tell me about a performance problem you solved.

the symptom →the real cause →the discipline

“The one that taught me the most wasn't a slow function — it was a transport choice.

Our client had a polling fallback: roughly forty registered queries, each re-fetched every tick, against a browser limit of six connections per host. Under polling the connection pool starves and everything queues behind everything else. It doesn't degrade gracefully, it collapses.

The fix was to make the streaming transport the only path, with no runtime branch — so a fallback can't be silently selected somewhere nobody tests. Polling stayed as an explicitly degraded mode rather than a default.

The discipline that came out of it: we keep a written list of the performance anti-patterns that have actually bitten us — serial file loops, opening a connection pool per item — and it's required reading before non-trivial changes. Most performance work isn't clever optimization; it's not re-committing a mistake someone already paid for.”

“How do you catch regressions?”
A performance leg in the release gate, so it blocks promotion instead of being noticed a month later.
Q · TradeoffsStandard

How do you decide when to take on technical debt, or ship something imperfect?

it depends on one thing →our answer →the boundary

“It depends entirely on whether you have users, and I think people apply the wrong default.

We don't have users yet. In that state the expensive mistake is caution — a compatibility shim, a deprecation alias, a half-fix to dodge churn. Each one is permanent debt taken on to protect nobody. So the bias is: if the right fix is a breaking change, make it now, completely.

The boundary is real though, and I'd change that answer the day someone depends on us — at that point breaking is a real cost rather than a hypothetical one. What I'd keep either way is the distinction between a fix and a mitigation. Restarting to clear it, widening a timeout, retrying around it — those are stopgaps, and they're fine as long as you say so out loud and the real fix has an owner. The failure is a mitigation recorded as a resolution.”

Q · TeamworkYour weakest spot — prepare it

Tell me about working on a team. / How do you handle code review?

The one question where a solo-built project is a liability. Don't invent colleagues — reframe honestly, then land on what transfers. Rehearse this one; it's the likeliest place to sound thin.

be honest →what it forced →what transfers

“I'll be straight that a lot of my recent work has been asynchronous, coordinating automated systems rather than a room of engineers — but it forced two habits I'd bring to a human team.

The first is writing for someone who can't ask you a clarifying question. When the other party can't come back to you, ambiguity doesn't get resolved, it gets guessed. That made me much more disciplined about stating scope, constraints and what I'd already ruled out, up front.

The second is about review. The most useful thing I can hand a reviewer is the evidence, not the conclusion — what I ran, what it returned, what I couldn't determine. ‘This works' asks them to trust me; ‘here's the failing case before and after' lets them check me. And saying what I couldn't establish turns out to matter more than what I could — silence there reads as confidence you don't actually have.

The related thing I've had to be deliberate about is that a decision affecting other people has to be written where they'll find it, not said once. I've watched the same argument reopen three times because the resolution only ever existed in a message.”

“How do you take critical feedback?”
Point at the retraction story — you've publicly corrected your own wrong finding and changed process because of it. That's a better answer than any claim about being open to feedback.
“Have you mentored anyone?”
If not, say so and pivot to the closest true thing: you write the conventions other people follow, and you learned that a convention nobody enforces isn't a convention — which is really a lesson about how people actually behave under deadline.
Q · Your turnAlways asked

Do you have any questions for us?

Free points, and the most commonly wasted moment in an interview. Ask two or three that only someone who has built this would think to ask. Never “what's the culture like.”

  • “How do you evaluate whether an AI system is working — is there a verification standard it has to meet, or is that case by case right now?” Signals that you think in terms of assurance, and it tees up everything you know.
  • “When an AI-assisted change enters an engineering pipeline, what's the audit trail, and who signs off?” Exactly the defense-prime concern, asked from the inside.
  • “What's the failure mode you're most worried about as adoption scales — wrong output, or nobody being able to explain the output?” A real question, and their answer tells you what the job actually is.
  • “Where does the platform team's responsibility end and a consuming team's begin?” Shows you've thought about platform work as a product with users.
  • “What would make you say, six months in, that this hire went really well?” Best closing question there is — it's a request for the success criteria, and you can restate them back.

The through-line — say it if you only get one impression

Every answer in this bank is the same shape: a rule that mattered, enforced by the system instead of documented and hoped for. Locks at the write path, evidence before done, a grader who structurally cannot be the author, a build that fails when prose disagrees with code. That is what a platform engineer is for, and it is the through-line worth leaving them with.

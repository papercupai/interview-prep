Domain vocabulary to have ready (general, not claims about SCARCE)
  • Security token: a token that is a security under US law. Offerings typically rely on an exemption such as Reg D 506(c) (accredited investors, verified) or Reg A+. Secondary trading needs a registered venue, usually an ATS run by a broker-dealer.
  • Transfer agent: the registered party that keeps the official ownership record. Whether the chain or the transfer agent's books are the record of truth is a key architecture question.
  • Books and records: regulated parties have retention rules (e.g. SEC Rule 17a-4 for broker-dealers, Rule 204-2 for advisers). That is why “audit trail” here means immutable, retained and producible on request, not just “we log things.”
  • Custody and keys: who holds the keys to mint, burn and transfer, and whether they use MPC or multisig. Agents should never hold signing authority alone.
  • Allocator expectations: family offices and RIAs usually ask vendors for SOC 2 Type II, documented incident response, and evidence of access reviews.

Use these to ask good questions. Don't assert which exemption, chain or partners SCARCE uses; find out.

Spoken draft · about 90 seconds

The principle, then the mechanism, then what you'd change

“The rule I ended up with was: autonomy in proportion to how reversible the action is and what a mistake costs. A wrong match meant we bought or listed the wrong item. That costs money and marketplace account health, while a missed match only costs an opportunity. So the system was allowed to act alone inside an envelope, and anything outside it went to a person.

Concretely: purchasing ran under a hard spend cap with a dry-run mode, and dropped anything we couldn't expect to sell at least weekly or that was too close to an item we already had. For ad bids the steps were deliberately asymmetric: cut spend immediately, raise it slowly, with a hard ceiling on the cost-of-sale target. Exceptions were human: operators could override a product's specs or blacklist it, and the system had to respect that.

What I'd do differently, and what I'd bring to Orchestra: make those thresholds earned, not guessed. Measure how often the human would have made the same call, widen autonomy only on evidence, and narrow it automatically after an incident. I'd also build the labelled set I never had, so the threshold is a measured precision, not a feel.”

Replace “hard spend cap” with your actual figures if you have them. If David remembers a different mechanism (repricing floors, order-volume limits, account-health stops), use your real one; the structure still works.

Don't oversell: no LLM made these decisions at Store Wolf. It was rules, ranking and a gradient-boosted pricing model. That's a strength here: deterministic, inspectable limits around automated decisions are exactly what an agent governance layer needs.

Governing Orchestra: who may act, within what limits, recorded how

A proposed design, not a claim about SCARCE's system. Ask what Orchestra's agents actually do first, then fit the tiers to their workflows.

Enforcement

Policy at the point of action

Every agent action goes through one dispatcher that checks identity, tier, envelope and approval before it runs, and denies by default. Prompts are not a control. Agents get their own identities and short-lived credentials (separate IAM roles), never shared keys.

Papercusp parallel: default-deny for tools with no declared gate, a per-role allowlist, a per-principal capability check, and a protected floor (raw secret reads, process kills) no autonomous role can use. G1.

Ratification

Approval that means something

The approver sees the exact action, the reason, the cost and the blast radius. Approval binds to that payload; if the agent changes it, the approval lapses. A resumed job must not repeat the write. Approve, edit, reject and cancel are all first-class options.

G4 · Human control. Papercusp's own default: agents propose and ask before executing unless an explicit, registered “auto” grant is on, and forcing a deploy past a red gate needs explicit owner sign-off.

Auditability

A chain from intent to receipt

Record a durable event before execution and a correlated outcome after: actor, agent and policy versions, inputs (or references), decision, approval, tool result. Denied attempts are recorded too. Logs are write-once (e.g. CloudTrail plus S3 Object Lock), access-controlled and retained per the regime. An uncertain timeout stays uncertain until reconciled.

G7 · Governance.

Earned autonomy

Thresholds that move on evidence

An action class can be promoted from “ratify” to “automatic within an envelope” after N ratified decisions with a high agreement rate and a bounded worst case. It's demoted automatically after an incident or drift. That's the Store Wolf lesson turned into a process, and it gives the GRC lead a documented rationale for every autonomy level.

When an agent has already acted wrongly

Contain: suspend that agent version and revoke its credentials; the kill switch has to be enforced by workers, not just a UI button. Reconcile each in-flight action by its stable ID against the downstream receipt; a timeout doesn't tell you whether it happened. Compensate where the business allows (you can't un-send an investor email). Then preserve evidence, fix the originating defect, and add a test for the failure class. G8 · Failure containment.

Non-deterministic agents still get SLOs

Infrastructure SLOs (availability, latency) aren't enough for agents. Add outcome SLOs: share of proposals ratified unchanged, escalation rate, false-action rate. An agent that starts escalating everything is “healthy” on CPU and broken on every metric that matters. Gate agent releases like code: pinned model and prompt versions, an evaluation gate, a canary, a one-step rollback. Lilly reliability prep has the long version.

08 · They asked applicants for this

A rollback executed under pressure

Their exact prompt: “what broke, what you rolled back, and what you changed afterward.” Pick a real incident you personally ran. The structure matters as much as the drama.

Structure · 60–90 seconds

Four beats

  1. What broke: the symptom, who or what was affected, and how you found out.
  2. The decision: why rollback rather than fix-forward. What you rolled back to, and how you knew that target was good.
  3. What the rollback couldn't undo: data written, messages sent, orders placed. How you reconciled them.
  4. What changed after: the guard that makes this class of failure fail loudly or roll back by itself next time.
Candidate · Papercusp releases

When the rollback itself failed

Papercusp deploys green builds to a separate release checkout with a health check and automatic rollback. A known failure mode: when the dev-box restart was withheld, the rollback crash-looped on MODULE_NOT_FOUND. Recovery was to stop the service and redeploy without the drain. Afterwards the required setting was documented and the recovery steps written down. The lesson: rehearse rollback itself, because the rollback path is code too.

Use this only if you were the one at the keyboard. Otherwise use a Store Wolf incident (a bad price push, a feed or listing change, a deploy) with real stakes.

Supporting · silent failure

Six hours with nothing alarming

Auto-commit stopped landing while its routine kept “firing,” so about a hundred files sat undeployable for about six hours. The watchdog was blind because it watched the release gate, not the commit step. Rules that came out of it: a watchdog must not depend on what it watches, and you choose the robust signal. N9 · Incident.

Regulated twist to volunteer: “In a financial workflow I'd separate code rollback from state. Reverting the service doesn't revert a token transfer or an investor notice. So every side effect needs an idempotency key and a reconciliation path, and schema changes go expand-then-contract so a code rollback never breaks the database.”

09 · Founder questions · 30–90 seconds each

Answers to rehearse

Practice prompts, not a known script. Lead with the answer, give one example, stop.

Why this seat, now that it's infrastructure rather than agentic?

“Because the hard part of agents in production is infrastructure and governance, not prompting. In Papercusp most of my effort went into the layer that decides what an agent may do, records what it did, and recovers when it's wrong. At Store Wolf it was limits around automated buying and bidding. Owning both the estate and the rules for autonomy is the version of this work where those decisions are actually enforced.”

Have you been the only one responsible for a production estate?

“Yes. At Store Wolf it was a two-engineer team, and I owned the software from inventory and pricing through fulfilment and day-to-day reliability. In September I rebuilt its deployment to be reproducible: a machine image, a provisioning script sized from measured data, secrets fetched from a secret manager under the machine's own identity, and a scanner that refuses to ship new plaintext secrets. On Papercusp I own the release gate, deploys and incident follow-up.”

Have one sentence ready on on-call reality: what woke you up, and how often.

How do you decide what an agent may do without a human?

“By reversibility and cost of error, not by how confident the model sounds. Reads and drafts are automatic. Reversible internal changes are automatic with an audit trail. External effects are automatic only inside a versioned envelope. Money movement, token operations and investor-specific communication are ratified against the exact payload. Then I make the boundary move on evidence: promote an action class after enough ratified decisions agree, and demote it automatically after an incident.”

What does auditability mean to you here?

“That we can answer, for any action: who or what initiated it, under which policy version, what evidence it had, who approved it, and what actually happened downstream, including denied attempts. Write-once storage, restricted access, the retention the regime requires, and reconciliation between the chain, the transfer agent and our own records. If a regulator or an LP asks, we produce it, not reconstruct it.”

AWS depth? Kubernetes?

“My infrastructure has been mostly on GCP and bare Linux: the Store Wolf rebuild on GCE, Papercusp's pipeline on our own hosts. On AWS I've used SQS, SNS and S3 as a consumer, but I haven't owned an AWS estate end to end. The patterns transfer directly: identity-based secrets, reproducible images, infrastructure as code, gated deploys. At your size I'd pick ECS on Fargate over Kubernetes until there's a reason not to.”

Honest and specific beats broad. If you hold any AWS certification or other real AWS work, add it.

Fintech, crypto, regulated experience?

“Not regulated finance directly. Store Wolf moved real money automatically under marketplace rules, where a bad decision cost cash and account standing. Papercusp is built around audit and evidence. I'd work closely with your GRC lead on the specific obligations, and design so the evidence is a by-product of normal operations rather than a quarterly scramble.”

Blockchain nodes and indexers?

I'd start with managed RPC providers and a fallback rather than running nodes, treat the indexer as a derived projection that can be rebuilt, and alert when on-chain state and the transfer agent's records diverge.

Part 1 · what the call is actually for

What the call actually decides

Nobody is evaluating your engineering on this call. A recruiter is clearing five gates, in roughly this order, and any one of them ends it.

  1. Does the résumé survive contact? They have a checklist drawn straight from the Must Have list — 7+ years, Python and TypeScript, production data pipelines, strong SQL, GCP/AWS. Your job is to make each box tickable in one sentence, and to be the one who volunteers where a box is only half-ticked. A gap you name yourself is a data point; a gap they discover is a flag.

  2. Comp alignment. $180k–$260k is an 80-thousand-dollar band, which means it spans Senior and Staff. The band is not the question — the level is. Get level discussed before you give a number, or you will anchor yourself into the bottom third by accident.

  3. Work authorization and logistics. The posting names E-Verify and Form I-9 explicitly. At an early-stage startup that is not boilerplate — it is a real screening step and it will come up. Volunteer it early and flatly; hesitating on this question reads as a problem whether or not you have one.

  4. Motivation and flight risk. Early-stage companies are afraid you want a big-company job and will leave when one appears. “I want to work on interesting problems” does not address that fear. Something specific about this stage and this problem does.

  5. Can they retell you in four lines? The recruiter will summarise you to the hiring manager in three or four sentences, from memory, hours later. Everything that survives that retelling is what you get evaluated on. Decide the four lines yourself — see §06 — rather than letting them pick.

The asymmetry worth remembering: gates 2, 3 and 4 are the ones that actually end most screens, and none of them are about engineering. Prepare them at least as hard as the technical narrative.

Part 2 · the first ninety seconds

The opener

Open · universal Always first ~90 sec

“Tell me about yourself.”

what you do now → the unusual constraint → why it maps here → what you want

A version tuned to a data platform role rather than a generic backend one. The move that matters is reframing agent infrastructure as what it actually is underneath — a high-volume ingestion and data-reliability problem:

“I'm a backend and platform engineer — the last [N] years building and operating a multi-tenant agent platform in TypeScript and Postgres. Concretely that's about fifteen thousand TypeScript files, nine hundred and eighty-eight SQL migrations on trunk, and roughly fifty tenant workspaces running side by side.

The part closest to this role is that underneath the AI framing it is fundamentally a data and reliability problem: ingesting high-volume event streams from a lot of heterogeneous producers into curated tables that everything downstream depends on — and keeping that trustworthy while about a hundred autonomous agents write to the same trunk continuously, with no feature branches and no pull requests.

I own that end to end — schema evolution, idempotency, retries and failure recovery, the gate that has to certify the data before it ships, and the tooling that makes onboarding a new tenant a single command instead of a project.

What draws me to Scowtt is that it's the same problem shape pointed at customer data, where the payoff is immediate and commercial rather than internal. I'm looking for a staff-level data platform seat where I own the architecture, and the early stage is the appeal, not something I'm tolerating.”

Don't

Lead with “AI agents.” In 2026 that phrase is free, everyone says it, and it primes the recruiter to file you as an ML person rather than the data-platform engineer they are hiring. Lead with the infrastructure claim and let the agents be the reason it is hard.

Don't

Go chronological. “I studied CS, then I joined…” spends your best thirty seconds on your weakest material and buries the one detail that makes you memorable.

Do

Use the real numbers. 988 migrations, ~15,000 TypeScript files, ~100 agents on one trunk are checkable, specific, and unusual. Vague seniority claims are what every other candidate says.

Do

End on what you want. It answers the flight-risk gate before it is asked, and it gives the recruiter the closing line of their summary.

Fill [N] before the call. You need one number for years of production experience and you should not be computing it live — the posting's bar is 7+, so know whether you clear it comfortably or exactly.

Part 4 · the three that will get probed

The three gaps

You have one Must Have gap, one Must Have half-gap, and one domain gap. Rehearse all three as sentences. An unrehearsed gap answer is where candidates either oversell — which unravels in round two — or apologise, which reads as weakness.

Gap 1 — Cloud and warehouses. gap The real one. They want GCP/AWS operating experience and warehouse integration, and the honest evidence is one BigQuery billing-export reader.

Say

“I should be straight about the shape of my cloud experience. I've built and operated data infrastructure at scale, but on self-managed Postgres rather than on a warehouse — the closest thing is a BigQuery billing-export integration. So if you're looking for someone who has run Snowflake or Redshift in production, that isn't me today. What I'd argue transfers is the part that's actually hard: schema evolution across nine hundred migrations, idempotent ingestion, and the reliability work. Picking up a warehouse's dialect and cost model is weeks; the discipline around it is years.”

Don't

Say “I've worked with BigQuery” and leave it there. It is technically true and materially misleading — a billing-export reader is not a data-platform integration, and the follow-up question exposes it immediately. The cost of being caught overselling on a Must Have is the whole pipeline.

Gap 2 — Python depth. partial They ask for strong hands-on in Python and TypeScript. Your TypeScript is overwhelming; your Python is working proficiency.

Say

“My production depth is in TypeScript — that's where the fifteen thousand files are. I'm fluent in Python and use it for tooling and data work, but I'd call it working proficiency rather than my primary production language, and I'd rather tell you that now than have it surface in a pairing session. Given the role is backend-and-data, I'd expect to be writing more Python here and I'm looking forward to that.”

Don't

Claim symmetry. “Strong in both” is the answer that gets you a Python pairing round you then underperform in. Calibrated honesty on a language is cheap; being caught is not.

Do

Spend an hour before the technical round on the Python data idioms you'd actually be asked about — generators for streaming large files, itertools, chunked batching, concurrent.futures, retry with backoff. See Python Gotchas.

Gap 3 — CRM / mar-tech domain. gap Nice-to-have only, so it costs little — provided you convert it into interest rather than absence.

Say

“I haven't integrated Salesforce or HubSpot. What I have done a lot of is the generic version of that problem — building ingestion against many third-party APIs that each have their own auth, rate limits, pagination, schema drift and idea of what 'incremental' means, and making the framework absorb that so each new source isn't a new project. From the outside, CRM and ad platforms look like the same problem with worse APIs. Is that roughly right, or is there something specific about them I should know?”

Do

End on the question. It converts a gap into a conversation, and it gets the recruiter talking about the actual integration surface — which is information you want before the hiring-manager round.

Part 5 · motivation

Why Scowtt, why this role

This answers the flight-risk gate. Generic enthusiasm fails it. Three specifics from the posting worth naming, because they show you read past the title:

  1. Their stated success metric is a platform metric, not a feature metric. “Customer data can be onboarded and activated significantly faster with less manual engineering work” is the first thing on their What Success Looks Like list. That is precisely the kind of leverage work you already do — onboarding as a single verb rather than a project. Say that the metric is why the role is interesting.

  2. The ambiguity clause is real, not decoration. They say twice that they want someone who takes an ambiguous problem and drives it independently. At most companies that is filler; at an early-stage startup with no platform yet it is the actual job. You've been operating without a spec for years — that is the match.

  3. The range spans two levels. An $80k band means the level is genuinely open, which means the scope is negotiable. That is worth more than the number: at this stage you can shape what the platform becomes rather than inherit it.

What not to say: “I'm excited about AI.” Everyone interviewing at an AI startup in 2026 says this, and it signals nothing. Your differentiator is that you've built the boring infrastructure underneath the AI — say that.

Part 6 · the differentiators

Four sentences only you can say

The recruiter will compress you into three or four lines for the hiring manager. Decide them now. Each of these is concrete, checkable, and hard for another candidate to reproduce — that combination is what survives a retelling.

1 · The constraint line. “About a hundred autonomous agents commit into one shared trunk continuously — no feature branches, no pull requests — and a gate has to certify that trunk before anything deploys.”

Why it lands: it is an unusual constraint, obviously real, and invites exactly one follow-up — how? — which is your strongest material.

2 · The schema-evolution line. “Nine hundred and eighty-eight migrations on trunk, expand-migrate-contract enforced, no runtime DDL — and the migration number comes from an atomic allocator, because with concurrent authors two people picking the next number by eye will collide.”

Why it lands: their posting names schema evolution explicitly. The allocator detail proves you have operated this rather than read about it — it is the kind of thing only experience produces.

3 · The reliability line. “There is exactly one liveness oracle behind every surface, so no two dashboards can disagree about whether something is alive. Before that, 'is it running' had four answers depending on which tool you asked.”

Why it lands: it is a data-consistency argument wearing operations clothing, which is exactly this role. It also shows you fix classes of problem rather than instances.

4 · The onboarding-leverage line. “Onboarding a new tenant is a single command — repo URL in, fully configured workspace out. It used to be a checklist somebody worked through by hand.”

Why it lands: it is a near-verbatim restatement of their first success metric. If only one line survives to the hiring manager, this is the one you want it to be.

Part 7 · the number

Comp & logistics

The band is $180k–$260k base. That spread is Senior at the bottom and Staff at the top. Answer the level question first — it is worth more than anything you can negotiate afterwards.

Say

“I saw the range is one-eighty to two-sixty, which I read as spanning Senior and Staff. Before I give you a number — what distinguishes the two here, and which one is this search actually for? I'd rather calibrate to the level than pick a number out of the air.”

Then

If they push for a number anyway: “For a Staff data-platform seat with the ownership described, I'd be looking at the upper part of that range — call it [X] — with the equity conversation mattering as much as the base at this stage.” Pick [X] before the call. Do not compute it live.

Don't

Give your current salary, or a number below $200k, before level is settled. The band's midpoint is $220k; anchoring under it for a Staff scope is the most expensive thirty seconds of the process.

Equity — ask these four, in this order. “Attractive startup equity” is unfalsifiable until you have numbers:

  1. How many shares, and what percentage of fully-diluted does that represent? A share count without a denominator is meaningless.

  2. What was the last 409A, and when was the last priced round?

  3. Standard four-year with a one-year cliff, or something else? Is there an extended post-termination exercise window?

  4. Are there refresh grants, and what does the pool look like after this round of hiring?

Work authorization. The posting names E-Verify and Form I-9. Volunteer your status in the logistics part of the opener, flatly and in one sentence, before they ask. Hesitation on this question creates a problem where there wasn't one.

Also settle: remote / hybrid / on-site and which office; start date and notice period; whether this is a new seat or a backfill; team size and who you'd report to.

Part 8 · your questions

What to ask them

Ask three or four. Each of these makes you sound like someone who has operated a platform, and each returns information you actually need:

  1. “What does the data platform look like today — is there a warehouse already, and which one?” Tells you whether Gap 1 is fatal or irrelevant. If they are pre-warehouse, your Postgres depth is the asset and you are choosing the warehouse, not inheriting it.

  2. “How many customer integrations are live in production, and how long does the next one take to add?” The single best question on this list. The gap between those two numbers is the job they are hiring for, and their answer tells you whether they understand that.

  3. “When a pipeline silently drifts — a customer changes a field and the data is wrong but not missing — who finds out, and how?” Silent data drift is the failure mode that matters and the one most startups have no answer for. It shows you know where the bodies are buried.

  4. “Is this a new seat or a backfill, and what decides Senior versus Staff?” Feeds the comp conversation and surfaces whether the scope is real.

  5. “What's the runway, and when was the last raise?” Entirely fair at an early-stage company, and asking it signals you are evaluating them. Ask it plainly, without apologising for it.

Do not ask anything answered by the posting. It is the cheapest possible signal that you did not read it.

“I've worked with BigQuery.” — true of a billing reader, misleading as a platform claim.

Do

“One narrow BigQuery integration — billing export. Not warehouse modelling.”

Don't

“I'm strong in Python and TypeScript.”

Do

“TypeScript is my production depth; Python is working proficiency and growing.”

Don't

“I build AI agents.”

Do

“I build the data and reliability infrastructure that agents run on.”

Don't

“I'm looking for a new challenge.”

“Your first success metric is onboarding speed. That's the work I've been doing — I want to do it where it's the product.”

Apologise for a gap. “Unfortunately I haven't…”

Do

State it flatly, then say what transfers and ask a question. Gaps stated calmly read as calibration; gaps apologised for read as weakness.

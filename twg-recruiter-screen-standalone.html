Part 1 · what the call is actually for

The five filters

The recruiter almost certainly cannot assess LangGraph, trajectory scoring, or your React architecture. They are working from a checklist written by the hiring manager, and their job is to pass along five or six people who are worth an engineer's hour. Treat every one of these as something to volunteer, not to wait to be asked.

The order matters

Filters 1–3 are logistics and they are binary. If one of them is a no, nothing else you say changes the outcome — so get them answered early, while you still have the goodwill to ask what flexibility exists. Candidates who save the location conversation for the end lose the whole loop over it.

  1. Filter 01 · the silent killer

    On-site in Santa Monica or New York

    The posting is unambiguous: workplace: on_site, two cities, relocation offered to the right candidate. This is the single most common reason a strong résumé gets a rejection with no feedback — the recruiter assumes you didn't read it.

    Say it in your first ninety seconds, unprompted: "I'm in [where you are] — I've read that this is on-site in Santa Monica or New York, and [I'm ready to relocate / I'm already local to X / here's what I'd need]." If you want hybrid, ask what the actual in-office cadence is after you've said you're willing, never instead of.

  2. Filter 02 · level

    Eight-plus years building production software at scale

    This is a Staff req at a $290–300k base. The recruiter needs a number and a title trajectory. Have both ready as one sentence: "[N] years, most recently [title] at [place], where I [one-line scope]."

    Staff level is about blast radius, not tenure. The posting names it directly — mentoring, code review, design feedback, engineering standards. Card N13 · Leading in the bank is your evidence; have one sentence of it here, not the whole answer.

  3. Filter 03 · money

    Compensation expectations

    You have an unusual advantage: the band is published. There is no reason to guess and no reason to go first with a number of your own. See §7 for the script.

  4. Filter 04 · the one real gap

    "Strong Java with Spring Boot"

    This sits in the required list, not the preferred list. It is the only line in the posting where your evidence is genuinely empty. §4 is the whole plan — read it twice; it is the most important page here.

  5. Filter 05 · the one you win

    "Real LLM and agent applications, not demos"

    That is the posting's own phrasing, and it is a screening question in disguise. Most applicants for an AI role in 2026 have a RAG side project and a LangChain tutorial. You run roughly a hundred agents committing into a shared tree, behind a ~650-tool MCP server, with an evaluation layer that can refuse a grader.

    Give the recruiter one concrete, repeatable sentence they can carry into the hiring manager's inbox verbatim. That sentence is the entire point of the call — see §6.

Part 2 · the first two minutes

The opener

Open · universal Always first ~90 sec

"Tell me a bit about yourself and what you're looking for."

Not an invitation to narrate your CV. It is the recruiter deciding, in the first thirty seconds, which pile you go in. Structure: where you are now → the one thing that makes you unusual for this posting → the logistics they need → what you want next.

now → the unusual thing → logistics volunteered → what you want

"I'm [N] years into building production systems — most recently [current role / context]. For the last [period] essentially all of my work has been agent infrastructure, which is why this posting caught me.

The short version: I build and operate a multi-agent engineering platform — on the order of a hundred agents working a shared codebase, coordinated through an MCP server exposing about six hundred and fifty tools, in a TypeScript and Postgres monorepo with a React operator console on the front. The part I'd most want to talk to your team about is the evaluation layer, because that's the thing in your posting that I think most people applying won't have actually built. We grade agent work against rubrics that require cited evidence, and the system structurally refuses to let an agent grade work in its own lineage.

On logistics, so it's not a surprise later — I've read this is on-site in Santa Monica or New York, and [your position on relocation / locality].

What I'm looking for is exactly the scope in the posting: owning agent design through the UI, and owning the evals that keep quality from regressing. I've been doing that for one system and I'd like to do it for a product with real users."

If they only want thirty seconds

"[N] years in production engineering, the last [period] building multi-agent infrastructure — about a hundred agents on a shared codebase, a 650-tool MCP server, TypeScript, Postgres and React. I care most about agent evaluation, which is the part of your posting I'd be strongest on. And yes, I've read it's on-site — [position]."

Don't

Start chronologically. "I graduated in… then I joined…" spends your best thirty seconds on your weakest material.

Do

Put a number in the second sentence. "About a hundred agents" and "six hundred and fifty tools" are what get repeated to the hiring manager. Vague scale gets dropped in the retelling.

Part 4 · the hard conversation

The Java problem

Gap · required line handle early ~40 sec

"How much of your recent work has been in Java and Spring Boot?"

There is no version of this where bluffing survives. The technical round will put you in a Spring Boot codebase and the claim collapses — with your credibility on everything else attached to it. The only winning move is to be first, brief, and specific about the plan.

Be clear-eyed about this

"Strong Java with Spring Boot" is in the required list. Some recruiters screen it as a hard gate and there is no answer that clears a hard gate you don't meet. What follows is designed to get you past a recruiter with any judgment, and to find out fast if this one has none — which is information worth having in week one rather than week five.

say it first → name what transfers → offer proof → re-anchor on the rare half

"Straight answer: I haven't shipped Spring Boot. My backend work is TypeScript and Node services and Python, against Postgres — so I'd be learning the framework, not the ideas. Dependency injection, layered services, a repository over a relational model, REST controllers, migration discipline — that's what I do daily, just in a different syntax.

Two things I'd say about that. First, I'm not asking you to take it on faith — I'm happy to do a Spring Boot exercise before the technical round, or to have that round be in Java on purpose. I'd rather be measured than believed.

Second, on the trade you'd actually be making. Your posting describes moving between a Spring Boot service, a Python agent layer and a React frontend in the same week. Of those three, one is the scarce skill. There are a lot of strong Spring Boot engineers; there are very few who've built agent orchestration from scratch and then built the evaluation harness that keeps it honest — and that's the half of the job I've already done at production scale. I'd expect to be useful in the agent and eval layers in week one and fully productive in the Java services inside a couple of months."

If they push

"Have you written any Java?"
Answer honestly with whatever the true answer is — [coursework / a past role / none]. If the answer is none, say none. A hedge here reads as a hedge on everything.
"How quickly could you get up to speed?"
Don't say "very quickly." Say what you'd do: "A Spring Boot service over Postgres with auth and tests is a weekend to build and a month of code review to build well. I'd want a senior Java reviewer on my PRs for the first quarter and I'd say so on day one."
"Why apply if you don't meet a requirement?"
"Because the two hardest lines in your posting are the two I'm strongest on, and the one I'm missing is the most teachable thing on the list. If that trade doesn't work for the team, I'd rather find that out now than at the offer stage."

Do this before the technical round, not after

Build one small Spring Boot + Postgres service — REST controller, JPA repository, Flyway migration, a couple of integration tests, containerised. A weekend. It converts "I could learn it" into "here's the one I wrote last week", and that is a categorically different sentence in an interview. It also means the words you use for DI and bean lifecycle are yours rather than borrowed.

Don't say

"Java, Spring, sure — I've worked across a lot of stacks." Every interviewer has heard this and it means no.

Do say

"I haven't shipped Spring Boot" as the first clause, in your own voice, before they ask. Volunteering a weakness buys you credibility that you then spend on the strengths.

MotivationNear-certain~45 sec

The recruiter is checking one thing: did you read the posting, or did you mass-apply? Cite two specifics from their text. Generic enthusiasm about AI is indistinguishable from spam.

"Two things, one about the role and one about the company.

The role first — it's rare to see evaluation written into the job rather than assumed. Your posting says the person owns 'the evaluation systems that keep agent quality from regressing as the platform grows', and that's the sentence that made me apply. Most agent teams treat evals as something to add once quality becomes a problem. Building the accountability layer as the platform is the work I've found most interesting, and I've done it the hard way.

On the company — the structure is unusual: decentralised business units with a central AI Solutions Group, across finance, insurance, media and sport. One line if they cut you off

"Your posting puts evaluation in the job description rather than treating it as an afterthought — that's the half of agent work I care most about, and it's rare to see it named."

"Why are you leaving your current work?"
Toward, never away. [Your reason] framed as scope: users, a team, a domain that isn't your own. Never a criticism of anyone.
"Are you interviewing elsewhere?"
Honest and brief. "A couple of processes in flight, nothing at offer stage" is enough. If there's a real deadline, say it — it's leverage only if it's true.

Part 6 · the differentiators

Four sentences only you can say

The recruiter will summarise you in three or four lines to the hiring manager. Decide now what those lines are. Each of these is concrete, checkable, and hard for another candidate to reproduce — that combination is what survives the retelling.

1 · The scale line

"About a hundred AI agents commit into one shared branch continuously — no feature branches, no pull requests — and a gate has to certify that branch before it deploys."
Why it landsIt is an unusual constraint, it is obviously real, and it invites exactly one follow-up: how? — which is Q1, your strongest answer.

2 · The evaluation line

"The system structurally refuses to let an agent grade work in its own lineage — including an agent it spawned itself. It's enforced at the write, not by policy."
Why it landsDirectly answers their trajectory-scoring requirement, and it's a design idea rather than a tool name. Tool names are cheap in 2026; this isn't.

3 · The agent-interface line

"With a 650-tool catalogue, every tool description is context you pay for on every call — so there's a hard character budget per tool, checked at registration and on every hot reload. Every budget breach on record came from editing a live tool, never from adding one."
Why it landsNobody who has only built demos has this problem. It is the fastest proof of "not demos" in the whole bank.

4 · The honesty line

"The failure mode with AI output isn't wrong answers — it's confident, plausible ones. So 'done' has to be a claim that gets substantiated, not asserted."
Why it landsIt's the thesis of the entire role, in one sentence, and it makes you sound like someone who has operated agents rather than launched them.

Part 7 · the number

Comp & logistics

CompCertain — they must collect it

"What are your compensation expectations?"

You are in the rare position of knowing the band before the call. $290,000–$300,000 base, plus a bonus described as part of the package rather than a maybe. The only error available to you is naming a number below it.

"You've published the band, so I'll just say it works — the $290 to $300 base is in line with what I'd be looking for, and I'd want to understand the bonus structure and equity, if any, before talking about a specific total. Is the band you posted the full range for this level, or does it move for someone at the top of it?"

Three rules

Never name a number first when the band is already public — you can only anchor yourself downward.
Never state your current salary. In California it is illegal for them to ask; if it comes up, "I'd rather anchor on the market rate for the role, and your posted band works for me" ends it politely.
Ask about relocation explicitly. The posting says support "can be provided to the right candidates" — that is a negotiable, and its value is real. Ask what form it takes, not whether it exists.

"What's your timeline?"
[Notice period + any competing deadline]. Precise beats eager.
"Are you authorised to work in the US? Do you need sponsorship?"
Straight answer, no elaboration. [Your status]
"Which office?"
Have a preference ready and a reason that isn't about you — proximity to the team you'd work with beats proximity to your friends. If you're genuinely open, say open; it's an advantage.

Part 8 · your turn

What to ask them

Ask three or four. A recruiter can't answer deep technical questions, so asking them wastes your slot and makes the recruiter feel useless — which they remember. Ask what a recruiter uniquely knows: process, people, and what the hiring manager is actually worried about.

"What does the interview loop look like from here, and how many stages?"
Always first. Tells you how much Java exposure is coming and when. Follow with: "Is any stage a live coding round, and in which language?" — that single question is worth the whole call.
"What's the hiring manager's biggest worry about the candidates they've seen so far?"
The best question available to you. Recruiters answer it honestly surprisingly often, and it tells you exactly what to emphasise in round two.
"Is this a new team or an existing one, and how many engineers are on it today?"
Establishes whether "Staff" means founding-the-practice or slotting-into-a-ladder. Very different jobs at the same title.
"The posting mentions Palantir Foundry as a plus — is that in the delivery stack for the client work?"
Shows you read past the requirements list. Also genuinely useful: if Foundry is central, this role is closer to client-facing platform delivery than product engineering, and you'd want to know that before round three.
"Which business units would this team be building agents for first?"
Finance, insurance, media and sport have very different regulatory and latency constraints. The answer tells you what the eval work actually has to prove.
"How firm is the on-site expectation day to day — is the team all in one office?"
Only after you've said you're willing (Filter 01). Asked in that order it's diligence; asked first it's negotiation, and you have no leverage yet.

Part 9 · calibration

Don't say / do say

Don't

Claim Java, Spring Boot, LangGraph, Braintrust, LangSmith, Foundry, or Swift because they appear in the posting. Every one of these is verifiable in round two, and one caught bluff retroactively discounts every true thing you said.

Don't

Describe Papercusp as "a side project" or "my own thing". Describe it as a system you build and operate, with the numbers attached. The scale is the argument; diminishing language throws it away.

Don't

Round the numbers up. ~100 agents, ~650 tools, 2,998 .tsx files, 920 migrations, 141 scenario tests. Precision is the whole advantage — an inflated figure invites a probe, and every probe after it lands on softer ground.

Don't

Explain the architecture to a recruiter. If you're two minutes into a technical answer on a screen, you've lost the room. Give the headline, then stop and let them ask.

Do

Volunteer the two logistics facts — location and level — inside the first two minutes, without being asked.

Do

Say "custom harness" about your evals, in those words. It's the phrase their own posting offers as the alternative to Braintrust and LangSmith, and it turns a missing tool name into a match.

Do

Name one thing you'd want a Java reviewer for. Volunteering the boundary of your competence is the most senior-sounding thing you can do on a screen, and it costs you nothing you weren't going to lose anyway.

Part 10 · around the call

Before & after

The night before

  • Fill every slot on this page. An unfilled slot is a stumble on the call — they are all facts only you hold.
  • Re-read Q8 · AI evaluation and N6 · Tool design. Those two carry this role; the rest of the bank is depth for later rounds.
  • Say the opener out loud twice, timed. Ninety seconds, not three minutes.
  • Re-check the four headline numbers against the tree so you're quoting today's figures, not August's.
  • Decide your relocation position before the call, not during it. Hesitating on this question reads as a no.

On the call

  • Logistics volunteered in the first two minutes: location, years, and that you've read it's on-site.
  • Java raised by you, not by them — briefly, with the offer of an exercise attached.
  • At least two of the four only-you sentences said out loud, with their numbers.
  • Three questions asked, starting with the loop structure and the coding-round language.
  • Confirm next steps and timing before you hang up, and get the recruiter's email.

Within twenty-four hours

  • Short thank-you email. Three sentences: one specific thing from the conversation, one line re-stating the agent-and-eval match, one confirming your logistics position in writing.
  • If Java came up as a concern, this is where you attach the Spring Boot repo — or say when it'll exist. Following through on a small offer is disproportionately persuasive.
  • Write down what the recruiter said the hiring manager worries about. That is your brief for round two.
